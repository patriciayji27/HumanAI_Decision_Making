# Attention, Memory, Salience & Cognitive Load

> How do interfaces shape what people notice, remember, and weigh in their decisions?

## Concept Overview

Even with perfect information and stable preferences, decisions can go wrong if the decision-maker's attention is misdirected, their memory is selective, or their cognitive load exceeds capacity. This stream studies the cognitive bottlenecks between information and choice.

Salience theory (Bordalo et al., 2012) formalizes how context-dependent attention distorts risk preferences — an option's payoff "pops" when it differs most from the alternative. Cognitive load theory (Sweller, 1988) describes how working memory constraints limit the complexity of information people can process. Choice architecture (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008) exploits these constraints to steer decisions through defaults, ordering, and framing.

For human-agent decision making, this stream is critical: AI explanations, dashboards, and advisory interfaces all compete for limited attention. More information is not always better. The question is how to design interfaces that direct attention to what matters most for the decision at hand.

## Evolution Timeline

**Foundations (1950s–1980s):** Simon (1955) introduced bounded rationality — the idea that cognitive constraints, not just preferences, shape decisions. Sweller (1988) formalized cognitive load theory, distinguishing intrinsic, extraneous, and germane load.

**Formalization era (2000s–2010s):** Gabaix (2014) developed the sparsity-based model of bounded rationality, where agents optimally allocate limited attention. Bordalo, Gennaioli & Shleifer (2012) proposed salience theory, offering an attention-based explanation for many prospect theory phenomena. Thaler & Sunstein (2008) synthesized decades of behavioral research into the nudge framework.

**Current frontier (2020s):** Enke & Graeber (2023) introduced "cognitive uncertainty" as a measurable construct linking attention to choice quality. Active research on: information overload from AI explanations (too many features, too much detail), attention allocation in AI-augmented dashboards, and whether AI advisors can serve as attention guides rather than just information providers.

## Open Questions

1. When AI systems provide explanations (feature importance, confidence intervals, counterfactuals), which format minimizes extraneous cognitive load while preserving decision-relevant information?
2. Can salience effects explain why people over-rely on or under-rely on AI advice in predictable, context-dependent ways?
3. How do memory limitations affect sequential human-AI decision making — does prior AI advice create anchoring effects that persist across unrelated decisions?
4. Is there a principled way to measure the "attention budget" a user has for AI-provided information in a given decision context?

# Uncertainty Communication: Visual, Textual, Agent-Mediated

> How should uncertainty be presented so people understand it and act on it well?

## Concept Overview

Stream 1 ([Belief Elicitation](../belief-elicitation)) addresses getting beliefs out of a person's head. This stream addresses the reverse problem: getting uncertainty information *into* a person's head — from a model, a forecaster, an expert, or an AI system — so that they understand it and use it appropriately in decisions.

The central finding of this literature is that **format is not neutral**. The same probabilistic information, presented as a percentage, a frequency, a bar chart, an animated sample, or a verbal expression, produces measurably different comprehension, different risk perceptions, and different decisions. This is not a minor nuance — the effect sizes are often large enough to reverse the sign of a treatment effect or flip a policy preference. A patient told "this drug has a 5% chance of serious side effects" and a patient told "out of 100 people taking this drug, 5 experience serious side effects" have been given identical information, but they frequently behave as if they received different news.

This makes uncertainty communication a *design problem*, not just a reporting problem. The communicator — whether a physician, a dashboard designer, a weather forecaster, or an AI system — is making choices about format, framing, granularity, and interactivity that causally affect the recipient's downstream behavior. Doing this well requires understanding what each format foregrounds and what it hides, how the recipient's cognitive profile (numeracy, domain knowledge, affective state) interacts with the format, and whether the goal is comprehension (understanding the uncertainty) or use (making decisions that appropriately incorporate the uncertainty) — because these are not the same thing.

### The Comprehension-Use Gap

A critical distinction in this stream, and one that is often overlooked, is between **comprehension** and **use**. Comprehension means the person accurately understands the uncertainty information: they can report back the probability, identify which outcome is more likely, or compare two uncertain options. Use means they incorporate the uncertainty information into their actual decision in a way that reflects its magnitude and structure.

These can come apart. Hofman, Goldstein, and Hullman (2020) showed that visualizations which improve comprehension of uncertainty do not always improve decision quality, and vice versa. A display that makes the distribution's shape crystal clear may not help the person figure out what to *do* about it. Conversely, a display that makes the key decision-relevant comparison salient (e.g., "this option is better 73% of the time") may sacrifice distributional understanding but improve choices.

This gap matters enormously for interface design. If the goal is education or informed consent (e.g., a patient understanding their treatment options), comprehension is paramount. If the goal is action quality (e.g., a trader making good portfolio decisions), use is what counts. Most research in this stream measures comprehension (because it's easier to test), but many applied settings care about use. The two may require different display strategies.

### Numeracy: The Great Moderator

Nearly every finding in this stream is moderated by **numeracy** — the person's ability to understand and work with numbers. Lipkus (2007) synthesized the evidence showing that low-numeracy individuals (roughly 30–50% of the general population, depending on the measure and country) struggle with probabilistic information in almost any format, but are disproportionately harmed by formats that require arithmetic or abstract probabilistic reasoning (e.g., conditional probabilities, relative risk reductions).

Peters, Västfjäll, Slovic, Mertz, Mazzocco, and Dickert (2006) showed that numeracy doesn't just affect comprehension — it changes the *nature* of the processing. High-numeracy individuals extract more precise meaning from numbers and derive more affective (emotional) meaning from numerical comparisons. Low-numeracy individuals rely more on non-numerical cues: gist, framing, trust in the source, and affective reactions. This means that a single uncertainty display cannot be optimal for all users. Formats that work well for high-numeracy users (precise confidence intervals, density plots) may confuse or mislead low-numeracy users, while formats that work for low-numeracy users (icon arrays, simplified categories) may feel inadequate to high-numeracy users.

This creates a design tension. Either the communicator chooses a format and accepts that it will work poorly for some segment of the audience, or the interface adapts — showing different representations to different users based on assessed or inferred numeracy. The latter is an active research frontier, especially in AI-mediated settings where the system might infer the user's numeracy from their questions and behavior.

## Visual Uncertainty Representations

The taxonomy of visual uncertainty displays is large and growing. Each format makes different aspects of a distribution visible and hides others. The major categories:

### Static Summary Displays

**Error bars and confidence intervals** are the most common scientific uncertainty display: a point estimate with bars extending to some interval (typically 95% CI, or ±1 standard error). They are compact, standardizable, and familiar to trained audiences. But they are chronically misunderstood. Belia, Fidler, Williams, and Cumming (2005) documented widespread misinterpretation of error bars even among published scientists: many cannot correctly determine whether two error bars indicate a statistically significant difference. The problem is partly that error bars encode a summary statistic (the interval) but suppress the underlying distribution, and partly that different bar types (SE, SD, 95% CI) look identical and carry different meanings. Hoekstra, Morey, Rouder, and Wagenmakers (2014) showed that even explicit textbook definitions of confidence intervals are misunderstood by a majority of researchers — most interpret a 95% CI as "there is a 95% probability that the true parameter is in this interval," which is incorrect under the frequentist interpretation.

**Fan charts** (also called cone-of-uncertainty displays) show progressively wider prediction intervals radiating outward from a central forecast, typically at several probability levels (50%, 80%, 95%). The Bank of England popularized them for inflation forecasts. They convey the widening of uncertainty over time and the approximate shape of the predictive distribution. But they encode probability through area, which is perceptually nonlinear, and the discrete intervals (50/80/95) create artificial "contour lines" that can suggest sharper transitions than exist. A well-known example of fan chart miscommunication is the National Hurricane Center's "cone of uncertainty" for hurricane track forecasts: research by Broad, Leiserowitz, Weinkle, and Steketee (2007) showed that many viewers interpret the cone as showing the *size* of the storm rather than the *range of possible tracks*, leading to dangerous underestimation of risk for locations near the cone's edge.

**Gradient/density plots** use color saturation or opacity to represent probability density continuously, avoiding the artificial boundaries of interval-based displays. A violin plot, for example, shows the full distributional shape as a mirrored density curve. These are informationally rich but assume the viewer can interpret density-as-area, which is nontrivial for untrained audiences.

### Frequency-Based Displays

**Icon arrays** (also called pictographs or Paling perspective scales) represent probabilities as proportions of a discrete set of icons — e.g., 100 person-figures, 5 colored to represent a 5% risk. They operationalize the frequency format of Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995) visually. Icon arrays have strong empirical support in medical risk communication: they reduce denominator neglect (the tendency to focus on the numerator and ignore the base), they make small probabilities tangible, and they improve risk understanding in low-numeracy populations (Garcia-Retamero & Cokely, 2013). Their limitation is that they work best for binary outcomes at a single probability level — they don't naturally convey distributions, uncertainty over time, or comparisons between multiple uncertain options.

**Quantile dotplots** (Kay, Kola, Hullman & Munson, 2016) extend the icon array idea to continuous distributions. Instead of coloring icons in a grid, they show a set of dots (e.g., 20 or 100) positioned at quantiles of the predictive distribution. Each dot represents an equally likely outcome. This makes the distributional shape visible through discrete samples while preserving the "countable" property that supports frequency-based reasoning. Kay et al. found that quantile dotplots improved decision quality under uncertainty compared to density plots and prediction intervals, particularly for tasks requiring comparison between uncertain alternatives.

### Animated and Interactive Displays

**Hypothetical outcome plots (HOPs)** (Hullman, Resnick & Adar, 2015) animate random draws from a distribution, showing one possible realization at a time in rapid succession. Instead of a static summary (an interval or density), the viewer sees a flickering sequence of concrete outcomes — experiencing the uncertainty as variability over time rather than interpreting it from a spatial encoding. The rationale is rooted in cognitive science: people are better at estimating frequencies from sequential experience than from spatial statistical graphics (Hasher & Zacks, 1984). Empirical evaluations show that HOPs can improve probabilistic estimation accuracy compared to static displays, particularly for tasks involving comparisons between distributions. Kale, Kay, and Hullman (2021) found that HOPs led to better probability-of-superiority judgments than error bars, intervals, or density plots.

The key advantage of HOPs is that they convert the uncertainty communication task from a *statistical reading* task (interpreting a graph encoding) to a *frequency estimation* task (estimating how often a pattern appears in a sequence), which aligns with the frequency-format advantage documented by Gigerenzer. The key disadvantage is that they require animation (not possible in all media), they are sensitive to frame rate and number of draws, and they may convey a false sense of randomness for distributions that are actually highly concentrated.

**Interactive exploration tools** go further, letting the user manipulate the display — adjusting parameters, filtering scenarios, or "playing" draws from a distribution on demand. These are increasingly common in data journalism (e.g., the New York Times election needle) and in decision support dashboards. The empirical evidence on interactivity is mixed: Hullman, Kay, Kim, and Shrestha (2018) showed that interaction can improve engagement and subjective understanding but does not always improve objective accuracy, and can sometimes give users an illusion of understanding that exceeds their actual comprehension.

## Textual and Verbal Uncertainty Communication

### The Verbal Probability Problem

People naturally communicate uncertainty in words, not numbers: "probably," "unlikely," "there's a good chance." But verbal probability expressions are notoriously imprecise and variable across individuals. Wallsten, Budescu, Rapoport, and Zwick (1986) elicited numeric equivalents for verbal probability terms and found enormous between-person variability: "probable" ranged from about 0.50 to 0.90 across respondents; "unlikely" ranged from 0.05 to 0.40. Budescu and Wallsten (1995) extended this work, showing that context, base rates, and the severity of the outcome all shift the numeric interpretation of the same verbal term.

This variability has real consequences. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) adopted a standardized verbal probability scale for its assessment reports (e.g., "very likely" = >90%, "likely" = >66%), precisely because inconsistent verbal communication was leading to misunderstanding of scientific consensus. Budescu, Broomell, and Por (2009) tested whether the IPCC guidelines improved understanding and found that even with explicit definitions, readers frequently misinterpreted the intended probabilities — "very likely" was interpreted as less certain than intended about 40% of the time. Budescu, Por, Broomell, and Smithson (2014) showed that adding explicit numeric ranges to verbal terms (e.g., "very likely [>90%]") significantly improved interpretation, suggesting that verbal-numeric hybrid formats outperform either alone.

The intelligence community faces the same challenge. The US intelligence community's standardized probability language (words of estimative probability) maps terms like "almost certain" to >95% and "unlikely" to 5–20%. Kent (1964) first proposed such a mapping; it remains in use but continues to produce systematic misinterpretation, particularly when the stakes are high and the reader has strong priors.

### LLMs as Verbal Uncertainty Communicators

Large language models communicate uncertainty almost exclusively through natural language — "I'm fairly confident," "this is likely but not certain," "there's a good chance." This makes the verbal probability problem acutely relevant for AI-mediated decision support. When an LLM says "I'm fairly confident that X," what probability does the user assign to X? Does it match the model's actual accuracy for statements expressed with that level of hedging?

The problem is compounded because LLMs are generally poorly calibrated: their expressed confidence (through linguistic hedging patterns) often does not correspond to their actual accuracy. A model that says "I believe X" with high fluency may be wrong 30% of the time, while a model that says "I'm not entirely sure, but possibly X" may be right 90% of the time. Users who take the model's verbal confidence at face value will systematically misjudge reliability. This connects to the calibration concerns in Stream 1 ([Belief Elicitation](../belief-elicitation)) and the trust dynamics in Stream 8 ([Agents as Advisors](../agents-as-advisors)).

### Numeric Precision and False Precision

There is a tension between precise and imprecise numeric communication of uncertainty. Reporting "there is a 73% chance of rain" conveys false precision if the forecast model's actual accuracy doesn't warrant the second significant digit. But reporting "there is about a 70–75% chance of rain" adds an interval that may be interpreted as additional uncertainty rather than as a statement about reporting precision. Joslyn, Nadav-Greenberg, Taing, and Nichols (2009) found that people treat numerically precise forecasts as more credible and act on them more, even when the precision exceeds the forecaster's actual resolution. Dieckmann, Peters, and Gregory (2015) showed that precision interacts with numeracy: high-numeracy individuals calibrate their trust based on justified precision, while low-numeracy individuals are more susceptible to false precision effects.

### Framing Effects: Frequency vs. Probability

The most influential finding about numeric uncertainty format is the **frequency advantage**: presenting statistical information as natural frequencies (e.g., "30 out of 1,000 women with a positive mammogram actually have cancer") rather than conditional probabilities (e.g., "the probability of cancer given a positive mammogram is 3%") dramatically improves Bayesian reasoning performance. Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995) demonstrated this in the context of medical diagnostic reasoning, showing that performance on Bayesian inference tasks jumped from around 15% correct with conditional probability format to around 50% correct with natural frequency format. Cosmides and Tooby (1996) provided an evolutionary argument: human cognitive machinery evolved to track frequencies of events in sequentially sampled environments, not to process single-event probabilities.

The frequency advantage has been extensively replicated (Sedlmeier & Gigerenzer, 2001) but also qualified. The benefit is strongest for problems requiring integration of multiple uncertain quantities (like Bayesian updating), and smaller for simple single-probability judgments. It depends on the frequencies being "natural" — maintaining the reference class structure of the sampling process. Artificially constructed frequencies (e.g., "3 out of every 100 chances") do not produce the same benefit (Brase, 2008). And the advantage is partly about reducing the computational difficulty of the task, not just about format per se — frequencies make the set-inclusion structure of Bayesian reasoning transparent.

The frequency vs. probability framing distinction has direct implications for how AI systems should report uncertainty. Should a medical AI say "this scan has a 73% chance of indicating pneumonia" or "out of 100 scans that look like this, about 73 are from patients with pneumonia"? The evidence suggests the latter is better for comprehension, especially for users with lower numeracy — but the former is more compact and conventional.

## Domain-Specific Communication Challenges

### Weather Forecasting

Weather forecasting is the most studied domain for uncertainty communication, partly because forecasts are verifiable and repeated, creating natural feedback. The National Weather Service's "probability of precipitation" (PoP) is perhaps the most communicated probability in the world, yet studies consistently show that many members of the public misinterpret it. Gigerenzer, Hertwig, van den Broek, Fasolo, and Katsikopoulos (2005) found that people interpret "a 30% chance of rain" differently depending on their implicit reference class: some read it as "it will rain 30% of the time," others as "it will rain in 30% of the area," and others as "30% of meteorologists think it will rain." Only the first interpretation matches the intended meaning.

Joslyn and LeClerc (2012) showed that providing forecast uncertainty information (e.g., "temperatures will be between 28°F and 35°F") improved protective action decisions compared to single-value forecasts ("temperature will be 31°F"), because the range display made the possibility of dangerous cold more salient. This is a case where uncertainty communication *itself* is the intervention — communicating that you're uncertain produces better decisions than communicating a precise-but-wrong point estimate.

### Medical Risk Communication

Medical risk communication is arguably the highest-stakes application. Patients and physicians must understand probabilities to give and receive informed consent, weigh treatment options, and interpret diagnostic tests. The challenges are compounded by the emotional valence of medical information, the base-rate neglect that afflicts both patients and physicians, and the fact that many patients have low numeracy.

Fagerlin, Zikmund-Fisher, and Ubel (2011) reviewed the state of medical risk communication and identified several principles: use absolute rather than relative risk (saying "the risk drops from 8% to 6%" rather than "a 25% reduction"), provide natural frequencies rather than conditional probabilities for diagnostic information, use visual aids (especially icon arrays) to make probabilities tangible, and present both benefits and harms in the same format to enable comparison. Garcia-Retamero and Cokely (2013) showed that visual aids — particularly icon arrays — substantially improve risk understanding in both high- and low-numeracy populations, though the magnitude of the benefit is larger for low-numeracy individuals.

### Financial Uncertainty

Financial uncertainty communication faces the additional challenge that the relevant distributions are often heavy-tailed, non-stationary, and subject to regime changes — properties that standard uncertainty displays (designed for roughly normal distributions) may misrepresent. The "risk" in a portfolio is not well-captured by standard deviation if the return distribution has fat tails. Communicating tail risk — the possibility of extreme but rare losses — requires special attention. Goldstein, Johnson, and Sharpe (2008) found that showing the full distribution of potential retirement outcomes (rather than just the expected return) changed investment choices substantially, making people more conservative. The format of tail information matters: showing the worst 5% of outcomes as explicit scenarios has a different impact than reporting a "5% Value-at-Risk number."

## Evolution Timeline

**Foundations (1970s–1990s):** The roots of uncertainty communication research lie in two distinct traditions. The first is the decision analysis tradition, where Howard, Raiffa, and colleagues emphasized that the way options and uncertainties are structured and presented to decision-makers is part of the analysis, not external to it — communication is integral to decision quality. The second is the risk communication tradition in public health, where the challenge of explaining medical risks to patients and the public drove early empirical work on format effects. Fischhoff, Slovic, and Lichtenstein (1978) established that people's risk perceptions are shaped by qualitative attributes (dread, controllability, familiarity) at least as much as by quantitative probabilities, suggesting that uncertainty communication must address affective as well as cognitive processing. In parallel, the development of probability forecasting in meteorology (Murphy, 1993) created the first large-scale, repeated application of uncertainty communication to the public.

**The frequency revolution (1995–2005):** Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995) and Cosmides and Tooby (1996) catalyzed a paradigm shift by demonstrating the frequency advantage — presenting statistical information as natural frequencies rather than probabilities dramatically improves Bayesian reasoning performance. This had immediate practical impact in medical education, legal reasoning, and risk communication guidelines. The program generated productive debate: critics argued the advantage was partly task-structural rather than format-based (Sloman, Over, Slovak & Stibel, 2003), while Gigerenzer and colleagues maintained that frequency formats align with evolved cognitive architecture. Regardless of the mechanism, the empirical fact — format causally affects performance — was established beyond dispute. Concurrently, research on numeracy (Schwartz, Woloshin, Black & Welch, 1997; Lipkus, 2007) established that individual differences in numeric ability moderate virtually all format effects, creating a fundamental design challenge.

**The visualization turn (2005–2018):** As data visualization matured as a field, researchers systematically investigated how different visual encodings of uncertainty affect judgment and decision-making. Spiegelhalter, Pearson, and Short (2011) provided an influential taxonomy and review of uncertainty visualization approaches, spanning risk ladders, icon arrays, distribution displays, and interactive tools. Ibrekk and Morgan (1987) had earlier shown that the choice between cumulative distribution functions and density displays significantly affects people's ability to extract probability information. Hullman, Resnick, and Adar (2015) introduced hypothetical outcome plots, bringing animation-based uncertainty communication into the mainstream. Kay, Kola, Hullman, and Munson (2016) developed quantile dotplots, bridging frequency-based reasoning with distributional display. Fernandes, Walls, Munson, Hullman, and Kay (2018) conducted a major meta-review of uncertainty visualization research, identifying that the effectiveness of different displays depends critically on the task (estimation, comparison, decision) and on the viewer's statistical sophistication. Padilla, Kay, and Hullman (2022) provided a cognitive science framework for why different visual uncertainty representations succeed or fail, connecting display design to perceptual and cognitive mechanisms (preattentive processing, ensemble coding, mental simulation).

**Current frontier (2018–present):** Three frontiers are active. First, **interactive and experiential uncertainty communication**: building on HOPs and quantile dotplots, researchers are developing interfaces that let users "experience" uncertainty through exploration — drawing samples, adjusting parameters, running personal simulations. The evidence on whether interaction improves decision quality (not just engagement) is still mixed. Second, **AI-mediated uncertainty communication**: as LLMs become decision support interfaces, the verbal probability problem becomes urgent at scale. LLMs communicate uncertainty in natural language, but their hedging patterns are poorly calibrated, variable across prompts, and interpreted inconsistently by users. Designing AI systems that communicate uncertainty in ways users actually decode correctly — perhaps through hybrid verbal-numeric formats, or adaptive displays that adjust to the user's apparent numeracy — is an active design challenge. Third, **evaluation methodology**: the field is grappling with *what to measure*. Should we evaluate displays on comprehension accuracy (can the user report the probability correctly?), decision quality (does the user make better choices?), appropriate trust calibration (does the user's confidence in a prediction match its actual reliability?), or behavioral change (does the user act differently than they would without the uncertainty information)? These metrics can diverge, and the choice of metric can reverse the ranking of displays (Hofman et al., 2020).

## Key Distinctions

**Comprehension vs. use**: Understanding the uncertainty (can you report the probability?) versus incorporating it into decisions (do you act as if you understand the risk?). Displays that optimize one don't necessarily optimize the other. Researchers and designers must decide which matters for their application.

**Format vs. content**: Changing the format (bar chart vs. icon array vs. text) while holding the content constant (same probability information) causally affects understanding and decisions. This means "just communicating the facts" is not a well-defined goal — the way you communicate them is part of the message.

**Frequency vs. single-event probability**: Natural frequencies ("30 out of 1,000") versus single-event probabilities ("3%") recruit different cognitive processes and produce different performance, especially on Bayesian inference tasks. The distinction runs deeper than notation: frequencies preserve the natural sampling structure, while probabilities require the listener to construct or imagine the reference class.

**Static vs. dynamic representations**: Static displays (a chart, a table) commit to a single visual encoding and require the viewer to interpret it. Dynamic displays (HOPs, interactive tools) present uncertainty as variation over time or across user-generated samples, potentially engaging experiential processing rather than analytic processing. Whether dynamic is better depends on the task, the user, and whether "engagement" translates to "accuracy."

**Risk communication vs. uncertainty communication**: Risk communication traditionally focuses on communicating the probability and severity of a specific harmful outcome (e.g., side effects of a drug, probability of a natural disaster). Uncertainty communication is broader: it includes communicating predictive distributions, model confidence, parameter uncertainty, and ambiguity. All risk communication is uncertainty communication, but not all uncertainty communication is about risk in the traditional sense. The distinction matters because risk communication research emphasizes affective responses and protective behavior, while uncertainty communication research emphasizes cognitive comprehension and distributional thinking.

**Precision vs. accuracy vs. calibration in communication**: A forecast can be precise (73% chance of rain), accurate (close to the true probability), and well-calibrated (73% forecasts verify 73% of the time). Communicating precision that exceeds accuracy creates false confidence. Communicating uncertainty about the forecast itself (second-order uncertainty) is important but cognitively expensive.

## Cross-References

- **[Belief Elicitation](../belief-elicitation)**: Elicitation and communication are two sides of the same coin — the format effects that distort communication also distort elicitation. Verbal probability expressions are studied in both streams, from different directions: elicitation asks how people *encode* beliefs into words, communication asks how people *decode* words into beliefs. The variability documented by Wallsten et al. (1986) and Budescu and Wallsten (1995) afflicts both processes.
- **[Utility & Decision Quality](../utility-decision-quality)**: How uncertainty is communicated affects the *quality* of resulting decisions. If a display makes one outcome's risk more salient, it may trigger prospect-theoretic effects (loss aversion, certainty effect) that would not arise with a different display of the same information. Communication format is an underappreciated source of framing effects.
- **[Attention, Memory & Salience](../attention-memory-salience)**: Every uncertainty display competes for attention and is processed through the same cognitive channels that are subject to salience effects, cognitive load limitations, and memory constraints. A beautiful, information-rich uncertainty visualization is worthless if the decision-maker's attention is consumed by other interface elements.
- **[Sampling & Representation](../sampling-representation)**: HOPs and quantile dotplots are, in effect, visual implementations of sampling-based reasoning. They convert the communication task from "interpret this statistical summary" to "estimate the frequency from these samples," leveraging the cognitive advantages of experience-based processing documented in the description-experience gap literature.
- **[Agents as Advisors](../agents-as-advisors)**: AI-mediated uncertainty communication is a new and rapidly growing area. LLMs communicate uncertainty verbally by default, raising all the verbal probability problems at scale. Designing AI advisors that communicate uncertainty well requires integrating insights from visualization, verbal probability research, and adaptive interface design.

## Open Questions

1. **Can we close the comprehension-use gap?** Many displays that improve understanding of uncertainty do not improve decisions under uncertainty (and vice versa). What display strategies jointly optimize both? Is this even possible, or is there a fundamental trade-off between transparent understanding and actionable simplicity?

2. **How should LLMs communicate uncertainty?** Verbal hedging is poorly calibrated and inconsistently interpreted. Should LLMs supplement verbal uncertainty with numeric estimates? Use standardized verbal scales? Provide uncertainty visuals within conversational interfaces? How should the system handle the tension between fluent, confident-sounding output (which users prefer) and well-calibrated, appropriately hedged output (which users need)?

3. **Can uncertainty communication be personalized in real time?** If numeracy is the great moderator, the ideal system would detect the user's numeracy level (from their questions, errors, or explicit assessment) and adapt its uncertainty format accordingly. Is this feasible? What does the adaptation look like — simpler displays for lower numeracy, or entirely different representational strategies?

4. **How should we communicate uncertainty *about* uncertainty?** Much real-world uncertainty has second-order structure: we're uncertain about the distribution, not just about the outcome. Model uncertainty, parameter uncertainty, and structural uncertainty all contribute to the total predictive uncertainty. Can interfaces convey this nested structure without overwhelming the user? Or is it better to collapse second-order uncertainty into first-order summaries (wider intervals, flatter distributions) and accept the information loss?

5. **What happens when uncertainty communication interacts with motivated reasoning?** In domains like climate, vaccines, and financial risk, the recipient may be motivated to discount or amplify the communicated uncertainty depending on their priors and values. Do different formats resist motivated reasoning better than others? Can interactive or experiential formats reduce motivated distortion by making the evidence harder to dismiss?

6. **How should tail risk be communicated?** Most uncertainty displays emphasize the center of the distribution (expected values, confidence intervals) and underrepresent the tails. But in many decision contexts (medical rare events, financial crashes, infrastructure failure), the tails are what matter most. What formats make tail probabilities salient without distorting the overall picture? How do you convey "this almost certainly won't happen, but if it does, the consequences are catastrophic" in a way that produces appropriate (not excessive) precaution?

Numeracy and Decision Making

Peters, Västfjäll, Slovic et al. · , 2006

Uncertainty Communication

Investigated how numeracy (the ability to understand and work with numbers) affects not just the accuracy of probability comprehension but the nature of processing itself. The key distinction: high-numeracy and low-numeracy individuals don't just differ in accuracy — they engage in qualitatively different cognitive operations when processing numeric information.

High-numeracy individuals (top quartile on the Lipkus numeracy scale) extracted both more precise meaning from numbers (more accurate probability estimates, better sensitivity to differences) and more affective meaning from numerical comparisons (stronger emotional responses to the difference between "10% risk" and "20% risk"). Low-numeracy individuals (bottom quartile) relied more on non-numerical cues: the gist of the message, the framing (gain vs. loss), trust in the source, and emotional reactions to the topic.

This has a critical design implication: a single uncertainty display cannot be optimal for both groups because they are engaging in qualitatively different cognitive operations. A precise confidence interval works for high-numeracy users (who extract meaning from the numbers) but fails for low-numeracy users (who may ignore the numbers and respond to framing cues instead). An icon array works for low-numeracy users (who can count and see proportions) but may feel inadequate to high-numeracy users (who want more distributional detail). Adaptive display — tailoring the format to the user's numeracy — addresses the core moderator, but requires assessing numeracy, which adds a step most interfaces don't include.

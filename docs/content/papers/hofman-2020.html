Prediction, Judgment, and Complexity

Hofman, Goldstein & Hullman · , 2020

Uncertainty Communication Decision Quality

Demonstrated the comprehension-use gap: uncertainty displays that improve people's understanding of a probability distribution do not reliably improve the quality of decisions they make based on that distribution. In prediction tasks, the format that produced the most accurate probability estimates (comprehension) was not the format that produced the best decisions (use).

The study used prediction tasks where participants viewed uncertain forecasts and made decisions based on them. Different display formats were evaluated on two independent metrics: (1) comprehension accuracy — can the participant correctly report the probability of a specific event? (2) decision quality — does the participant make choices that maximize expected utility given the uncertainty? The display format rankings differed across the two metrics, and in some cases, a format that was best for comprehension was mediocre or poor for decisions.

The gap arises because understanding a distribution and identifying the right action are different cognitive operations. Comprehension requires reading the display and extracting a probability. Decision-making requires comparing the extracted probability against the decision threshold, factoring in costs and benefits, and choosing. A display can be clear about the probability but unclear about what action it implies — or clear about the action but opaque about the underlying probability.

The practical implication: for applications where the goal is informed consent (e.g., medical risk communication), comprehension is the right metric. For applications where the goal is action quality (e.g., financial decisions, operational planning, AI-assisted decisions), decision quality is the right metric. Assuming one transfers to the other is not supported by the evidence.

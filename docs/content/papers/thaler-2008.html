Strong Canonical

Nudge

Thaler & Sunstein · , 2008

Attention & Salience

Synthesized decades of behavioral economics and psychology research into the "choice architecture" framework: the structure in which options are presented causally affects which option people choose, even when no option is restricted or removed. The core claim: there is no such thing as a neutral presentation — every display, ordering, and default is a design choice that influences behavior.

The most robust finding from the choice architecture literature is the default effect: people disproportionately stick with the pre-selected or status quo option. The most cited example: organ donation rates are roughly 85% in European countries with opt-out defaults (you are a donor unless you actively decline) and roughly 15% in countries with opt-in defaults (you are not a donor unless you actively register), holding cultural and institutional factors roughly constant (Johnson & Goldstein, 2003). Default effects are the strongest and most reliable nudge-type intervention.

Other interventions documented in the book — salience manipulations (making one option more visually prominent), framing effects (describing the same option in gain vs. loss terms), social proof (showing what others chose), simplification (reducing the number of options) — are real but typically produce smaller and more variable effects than defaults. The practical implication: for choice architecture interventions, defaults should be the first lever tried. Other nudges are supplementary.

The book's primary influence was in policy design (automatic enrollment in retirement savings, calorie labeling, organ donation defaults) rather than in producing new empirical findings. The concepts are heavily cited in human-AI interaction research, where the AI's recommended option functions as a default that the user must actively override.

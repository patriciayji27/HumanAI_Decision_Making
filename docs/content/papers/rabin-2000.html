Risk Aversion and Expected-Utility Theory: A Calibration Theorem

Rabin · Econometrica, 2000

Decision Quality Risk & Ambiguity

The specific contribution

A mathematical proof with devastating implications for expected utility theory. If a person turns down a 50/50 gamble of losing $10 / gaining $11 at any wealth level (a modest, plausible-sounding preference), then expected utility theory implies they should also turn down a 50/50 gamble of losing $100 / gaining any amount of money — millions, billions, any finite sum. The implied global risk aversion is absurd.

The argument

Expected utility theory explains risk aversion through diminishing marginal utility of wealth: the utility of an additional dollar is lower when you're richer. This is reasonable for large-stakes decisions. But for small-stakes gambles (losing or gaining a few hundred dollars), the stakes are too small relative to total lifetime wealth for diminishing marginal utility to produce the observed risk aversion. Any EU model that fits small-stakes aversion must have a utility function so concave that it predicts absurd large-stakes aversion.

Conclusion: loss aversion — not diminishing marginal utility — must be the primary mechanism for everyday risk aversion. This is precisely what prospect theory's reference-dependent value function provides.

Qualifications

Theoretical result — assumes EU theory is taken seriously as a global model of risk preferences across stakes. The CRRA (constant relative risk aversion) utility function is not the only EU specification; some variations can accommodate small-stakes aversion without absurd large-stakes predictions, though at the cost of other implausible features. The result is most relevant to applications that use EU as a normative benchmark.

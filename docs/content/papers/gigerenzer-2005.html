How People Interpret Probabilistic Weather Forecasts

Gigerenzer, Hertwig et al. · , 2005

Uncertainty Communication

Surveyed how residents of five major cities (New York, Amsterdam, Berlin, Athens, Milan) interpret the statement "there is a 30% chance of rain tomorrow." Participants chose among multiple interpretations of what "30% chance of rain" means.

Responses split into three distinct interpretations: (1) "It will rain 30% of the time" (i.e., 30% of the hours tomorrow will have rain) — the time interpretation. (2) "It will rain in 30% of the area" — the spatial interpretation. (3) "30% of meteorologists predict rain" — the forecaster-agreement interpretation. Only interpretation (1) is close to the actual definition (of days with these atmospheric conditions, 30% produce measurable precipitation), and even that is imprecise.

The misunderstanding was pervasive across all five cities, though the distribution of incorrect interpretations varied by location. The fundamental problem is that the reference class ("of days like this") is implicit in the probability statement. When the reference class is unstated, people import their own — and different people import different reference classes, leading to systematically different interpretations of the same number.

This is the most commonly communicated probability in the world (weather forecasts are issued daily to billions of people), and most recipients misunderstand it. The finding directly illustrates why uncertainty communication requires explicit reference class specification — which is precisely what natural frequency formats (Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, 1995) provide.

How Well Do Researchers Understand Confidence Intervals?

Hoekstra, Morey, Rouder & Wagenmakers · , 2014

Uncertainty Communication

Presented six statements about 95% confidence intervals to 120 researchers and 442 students and asked which statements were correct. All six statements were actually incorrect — they described common misinterpretations of CIs.

The most prevalent error: treating a 95% CI as "there is a 95% probability the true parameter is in this interval." Under frequentist inference, this is incorrect — a 95% CI means that if you repeated the experiment many times and computed a CI each time, 95% of those CIs would contain the true parameter. Any specific CI either contains the true parameter or it doesn't; there is no probability involved. The Bayesian interpretation (credible interval) does assign a probability, but a frequentist CI is not a credible interval.

The majority of both researchers and students endorsed at least one incorrect statement. Researchers — people who use CIs professionally in published papers — were not significantly better than students at interpreting them. The errors were not random; they reflected systematic Bayesian intuitions about what a CI should mean, applied to a frequentist construct that doesn't support those intuitions.

The practical implication: if trained researchers cannot correctly interpret confidence intervals, designing CI-based displays for non-expert users (patients, policy-makers, AI system users) is not justified by the evidence on how people actually process them. Alternative formats (icon arrays, quantile dotplots, natural frequencies) that don't require understanding the frequentist interpretation are likely to produce more accurate comprehension.

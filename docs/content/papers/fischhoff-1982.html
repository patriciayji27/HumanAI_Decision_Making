Subjective Confidence in Forecasts

Fischhoff & MacGregor · Journal of Forecasting, 1982

Uncertainty Communication Belief Elicitation

The specific contribution

Systematic study of how recipients interpret probabilistic forecasts — examining what people take probabilistic statements to mean rather than what they are intended to mean. Found that the same forecast phrase (e.g., "likely precipitation tomorrow") is interpreted differently depending on whether it refers to time, area, or ensemble agreement; and that recipients substitute different underlying meanings for the same probability value depending on context and prior beliefs.

Key findings

Forecasters and forecast recipients often operate with different implicit reference classes. A meteorologist saying "40% chance of rain" means probability integrated over space and time in a specific technical sense. Recipients interpret it as referring to how much of the day will be rainy, or how sure the forecaster is, or how much of the city will see rain — diverging systematically from the intended meaning.

This is the documented origin of what Gigerenzer et al. (2005) later showed still persists decades later: chronic misunderstanding of probability of precipitation. The problem is not numeracy; it is reference class ambiguity in the forecast statement itself.

Why it matters for AI

LLM-generated uncertainty expressions ("this is likely," "I'm fairly confident") carry exactly the same reference class ambiguity. The Fischhoff & MacGregor finding implies that the problem cannot be solved by educating users — it requires clarifying what the probability refers to in the statement itself, which is rarely done in AI output.

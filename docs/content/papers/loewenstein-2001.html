Risk as Feelings

Loewenstein, Weber, Hsee & Welch · , 2001

Risk & Ambiguity Uncertainty Communication

Proposed a dual-process model of risk evaluation: people assess risk through two parallel pathways — a cognitive ("risk as analysis") pathway that computes probabilities and expected values, and an affective ("risk as feelings") pathway that generates emotional responses to the prospect of harm or benefit. The two pathways can diverge, and when they do, the affective pathway typically dominates behavior.

Examples of divergence: a person can know the probability of a plane crash is negligible (cognitive assessment) but feel terrified of flying (affective assessment). A patient can know that a 5% surgical complication rate is low but feel that the risk is unacceptable when imagining themselves on the operating table. In both cases, the emotional response drives the actual decision more strongly than the cognitive calculation.

The affect pathway is influenced by factors that have no place in rational risk assessment: vividness (a risk that can be easily imagined feels more dangerous), immediacy (a risk faced now feels larger than the same risk faced later), personal connection (knowing someone who experienced the outcome amplifies the affective response). These factors explain why risk communication that is technically accurate can still fail to produce appropriate behavior: if the format fails to engage the affective pathway (e.g., abstract percentages), the cognitive assessment may not translate into action.

The implication for AI systems: presenting well-calibrated probability estimates may fail to produce calibrated user behavior if the user's affective response overrides the numeric signal. A user who sees "3% failure rate" and simultaneously observes the AI making a fluent, confident-sounding recommendation may feel the AI is reliable, regardless of what the number says.

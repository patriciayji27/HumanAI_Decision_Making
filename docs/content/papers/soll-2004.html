Overconfidence in interval estimates

Soll & Klayman · , 2004

Belief Elicitation

Investigated the cognitive process that produces overly narrow confidence intervals. The key question: is overconfidence in intervals driven by the same mechanism as overconfidence in binary judgments, or is it a distinct phenomenon?

Across multiple experiments, participants generated 90% confidence intervals for uncertain quantities (e.g., "Give a range such that you are 90% sure the true answer falls inside"). The authors decomposed the interval-setting process into two steps: (1) generating a central estimate (the "anchor"), and (2) adjusting outward from that anchor to set the upper and lower bounds.

The finding: the central estimates were well-placed — they were approximately unbiased, falling close to the true value on average. The problem was in step 2: people did not adjust far enough outward from their central estimate. The adjustment was systematically insufficient, producing intervals that were centered well but far too narrow. This is a specific instance of the anchoring-and-adjustment heuristic (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974), where the adjustment operation is effortful and people terminate it prematurely.

The implication is that overconfidence in intervals is not primarily a motivational phenomenon (overestimating one's knowledge) or a perceptual one (misperceiving the difficulty of items). It is a process failure in the adjustment step. This diagnosis suggests specific interventions: prompting people to think of reasons why the true value might be extreme, or explicitly asking for the best and worst plausible values before constructing the interval, should widen intervals and improve calibration. Some subsequent work has confirmed this (e.g., "consider the opposite" protocols).

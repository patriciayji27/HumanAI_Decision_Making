Calibration of Probabilities: The State of the Art to 1980

Lichtenstein, Fischhoff & Phillips · , 1982

Belief Elicitation

A comprehensive review that established overconfidence in calibration as one of the most robust findings in the judgment literature. The review synthesized studies from the 1960s through 1980 that asked participants to set confidence intervals for uncertain quantities or to assign probabilities to binary events and then assessed the correspondence between stated confidence and actual accuracy.

The central finding: when people set 90% confidence intervals for uncertain quantities (population of countries, historical dates, physical constants), the true value falls inside the interval only about 50% of the time. This was replicated across general knowledge, medical diagnosis, legal judgment, engineering estimation, and financial forecasting. The effect holds for both experts and novices, though the magnitude varies.

For probability judgments (assigning a probability to a binary proposition and checking whether the stated probability matches the observed frequency of being correct), the pattern is more nuanced. The hard-easy effect (Lichtenstein & Fischhoff, 1977) shows overconfidence on hard items (where accuracy is low) but underconfidence on easy items (where accuracy is high). This suggests confidence judgments are insufficiently regressive toward the base rate of accuracy — people don't adjust enough toward "I have no idea" when items are hard, or toward "I'm definitely right" when items are easy.

The review established the vocabulary and measurement framework (calibration curves, reliability diagrams, Murphy decomposition into calibration + resolution + uncertainty) that subsequent work uses. Soll & Klayman (2004) later identified the cognitive process: people anchor on their best guess and adjust outward to set interval bounds, but the adjustment is systematically insufficient.

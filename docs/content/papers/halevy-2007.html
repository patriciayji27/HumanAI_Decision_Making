Ellsberg Revisited: An Experimental Study

Halevy · Econometrica, 2007

Risk & Ambiguity

The specific contribution

Carefully separates two mechanisms that are conflated in the standard interpretation of Ellsberg-style ambiguity aversion: (1) true ambiguity aversion — disliking uncertainty about the probability itself — and (2) failure to reduce compound lotteries — treating a two-stage lottery differently from its single-stage equivalent even when probabilities are fully specified. Many subjects who appear ambiguity-averse in Ellsberg tasks do not actually reduce compound lotteries, suggesting the phenomenon is about probability integration, not ambiguity per se.

The compound lottery test

In the standard Ellsberg setup, the ambiguous urn is equivalent to a compound lottery: first, nature draws the composition of the urn, then a ball is drawn. Subjects who reduce compound lotteries should treat this the same as a known probability. Halevy found that the vast majority of subjects who display Ellsberg-type behavior also fail to reduce compound lotteries when the same problem is made explicit — suggesting ambiguity aversion and compound lottery non-reduction are the same phenomenon.

Qualifications

The interpretation that ambiguity aversion IS non-reduction of compound lotteries is contested — some authors argue both mechanisms are real and empirically dissociable. The compound lottery design may itself introduce confounds (the compounding may make the uncertainty feel more salient). The result challenges the need for dedicated ambiguity models if the underlying phenomenon is probability integration failure.

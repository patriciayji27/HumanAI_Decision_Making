Reference-Dependent Preferences and the Observed Violations of Expected Utility

Kőszegi & Rabin · , 2006

Decision Quality

Addressed a long-standing problem in prospect theory: the reference point. In the original 1979 model, the reference point is assumed to be the status quo, but this is often ambiguous or context-dependent. Kőszegi and Rabin proposed that the reference point is the person's rational expectation of the outcome they will receive.

The model works as follows: a consumer who expects to buy a product at $20 and pays $20 experiences no gain or loss. If they pay $15, they experience a $5 gain relative to their expectation. If they pay $25, they experience a $5 loss. Because losses loom larger than gains (loss aversion), the pain of the unexpected $5 surcharge exceeds the pleasure of the unexpected $5 discount.

This generates specific testable predictions: (1) Loss aversion affects behavior only when expectations are surprised — expected outcomes, even bad ones, are fully encoded into the reference point and produce no gain-loss evaluation. (2) The reference point adjusts to the entire distribution of possible outcomes, not just the most likely one. (3) In equilibrium, firms and consumers anticipate each other's behavior, and reference-dependent preferences create real economic effects (e.g., price stickiness, asymmetric demand responses to price changes).

The conceptual tension: the model assumes agents form rational expectations about outcomes, then evaluate deviations from those expectations using prospect theory — a model designed to capture bounded rationality. Whether the same agent can simultaneously be a rational expectations optimizer (for forming the reference point) and a bounded prospect-theoretic evaluator (for processing deviations) is debated.

Mixed Debated

Decisions from Experience Without Feedback

Fox & Hadar · , 2006

Sampling & Experience

Challenged the cognitive-processing interpretation of the description-experience gap (Hertwig et al., 2004). The argument: when you control for the actual information participants received in their samples (not just the objective probability), much of the gap disappears.

The key insight: a participant who drew 15 samples and happened to encounter a 10% event zero times (which has a ~21% probability of occurring with n=15) made a perfectly rational decision given their information — they had no evidence of the rare event. Their behavior looks like "underweighting" only if you evaluate it against the objective probability, which the participant never observed. If you evaluate it against their sample, their behavior is consistent with Bayesian updating on the information they actually received.

Fox & Hadar tested this by comparing choices in the experience condition to choices that would be predicted by a Bayesian agent who observed the same sample. The gap between description and experience shrank substantially (though did not fully disappear) after this correction. Camilleri & Newell (2011) later confirmed a small residual gap persists after controlling for sample information, suggesting both mechanisms contribute: most of the gap is sampling error, but a modest processing-mode difference may exist on top of it.

The practical implication: the description-experience gap is real as a behavioral phenomenon, but its theoretical interpretation matters for design. If it's mostly sampling error, the solution is ensuring users get enough experience (or supplementing experience with described base rates). If it's a processing-mode difference, the solution requires changing how the information is encoded, not just how much of it is provided.

Strong Canonical

Specious Reward: A Behavioral Theory of Impulsiveness and Impulse Control

Ainslie · Psychological Bulletin, 1975

Temporal Dynamics

The specific contribution

First systematic argument that hyperbolic discounting — not exponential discounting — characterizes how organisms value delayed rewards. Ainslie synthesized evidence from animal learning experiments showing that delay-of-reward curves are better fit by 1/(1+kD) (hyperbolic) than e−kD (exponential). The crucial behavioral consequence: hyperbolic discounting produces preference reversals as the delay shortens — a smaller-sooner reward is preferred once it becomes imminent, even though the same person preferred the larger-later reward when both were distant.

The preference reversal mechanism

Decision pointPreference under exponentialPreference under hyperbolic
1 month before choiceLarger-later rewardLarger-later reward
1 day before choiceStill larger-later (stable)May switch to smaller-sooner
Moment of choiceStill larger-later (stable)Smaller-sooner (preference reversed)

This reversal explains why people plan to diet but eat the cake when it's in front of them; why they plan to save but spend; why they commit to future exercise but don't exercise today. The same person holds inconsistent preferences across time — and this is not irrational confusion but a predictable consequence of hyperbolic delay sensitivity.

Legacy

Foundational for all behavioral economics work on present bias, self-control, and commitment devices. Loewenstein & Prelec (1992) provided the functional form for humans; O'Donoghue & Rabin (1999) built the formal economic model with sophistication vs. naïvety. The commitment device literature (Ariely & Wertenbroch on deadlines; Thaler & Benartzi on Save More Tomorrow) is a direct application.

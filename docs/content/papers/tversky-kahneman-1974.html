Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases

Tversky & Kahneman · Science, 1974

Decision Quality Belief Elicitation Attention & Salience

The specific contribution

Identified three primary heuristics people use when making probability judgments under uncertainty, and characterized the systematic biases each produces:

HeuristicMechanismKey bias produced
RepresentativenessJudge probability by similarity to a prototypeBase rate neglect; conjunction fallacy; insensitivity to sample size
AvailabilityJudge frequency by ease of recallOverestimation of vivid/recent/emotionally salient events
Anchoring and adjustmentEstimate by adjusting from an initial anchorInsufficient adjustment; anchor effects persist even with arbitrary starting points

Classic demonstrations: people judge that a description of a shy, orderly individual is more probably a librarian than a farmer — despite far more farmers than librarians (representativeness overrides base rates). Wheel-of-fortune anchors significantly shifted estimates of African countries' UN membership percentage (anchoring with arbitrary values).

Evolution of the framework

The heuristics-and-biases program has been substantially refined since 1974. The ecological rationality critique (Gigerenzer) argued that these heuristics are adaptive in many environments and only produce errors in ecologically unrepresentative lab tasks. Dual-process theory (Kahneman's own System 1 / System 2 framework) provided a cognitive architecture for when heuristics are versus are not engaged. The 1974 paper remains the foundational reference, but its framing as stable "biases" is now understood as overstated.

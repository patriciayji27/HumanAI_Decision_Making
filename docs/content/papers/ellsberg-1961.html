Risk, Ambiguity, and the Savage Axioms

Ellsberg · , 1961

Risk & Ambiguity

Proposed two thought experiments that demonstrated a systematic violation of the sure-thing principle (Savage's P2 axiom) of subjective expected utility theory — revealing that people's preferences are influenced not just by probabilities and outcomes, but by the degree of confidence in the probabilities themselves.

The classic three-color urn version: an urn contains 90 balls — 30 are red, the remaining 60 are black or yellow in unknown proportions. A ball is drawn at random. Consider two choices:

Choice 1: Gamble A (win $100 if red) vs. Gamble B (win $100 if black). Most people prefer A — they know the probability of red (1/3) but don't know the probability of black.

Choice 2: Gamble C (win $100 if red or yellow) vs. Gamble D (win $100 if black or yellow). Most people prefer D — the probability of "black or yellow" is known (2/3) while the probability of "red or yellow" is not.

Preferring A over B implies believing P(red) > P(black). Preferring D over C implies believing P(black or yellow) > P(red or yellow), which means P(black) > P(red). These preferences contradict each other under any consistent probability assignment — violating the sure-thing principle. The pattern cannot be explained by any probability weighting or risk aversion; it requires sensitivity to ambiguity (the absence of known probabilities) as a factor distinct from risk.

The paper coined the distinction between risk (known probability) and ambiguity (unknown probability) that became foundational to subsequent theoretical work. Gilboa & Schmeidler (1989) axiomatized maxmin expected utility as a formal response; Klibanoff, Marinacci & Mukerji (2005) developed smooth ambiguity preferences; Fox & Tversky (1995) added the source-preference dimension.

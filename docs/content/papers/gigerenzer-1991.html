Probabilistic mental models: A Brunswikian theory of confidence

Gigerenzer, Hoffrage & Kleinbölting · , 1991

Belief Elicitation

Proposed the Probabilistic Mental Models (PMM) theory as an ecological rationality account of overconfidence. The central argument: the systematic overconfidence documented by Lichtenstein, Fischhoff & Phillips (1982) is partly an artifact of how experimenters select test questions, not purely a cognitive bias of the participants.

The key insight concerns item selection. Typical calibration experiments use items selected to be difficult and informative for research purposes — cities with surprising populations, dates that are counterintuitive, facts that educated people get wrong. These items are systematically unrepresentative of the person's natural information environment. If a person's internal confidence model was calibrated to the frequency of being correct in their everyday environment (where most questions they encounter are ones they can answer), then importing that calibration to an artificially difficult test produces apparent overconfidence — even if the person is perfectly calibrated in their natural ecology.

Gigerenzer et al. formalized this with the PMM model: when asked a two-alternative question (e.g., "Which city is larger, Hyderabad or Islamabad?"), the person searches for a cue that discriminates (a recognition cue, a knowledge cue). The confidence in the answer is derived from the validity of that cue in the person's reference class. If the reference class is "German cities I know something about" and the test items are drawn from "obscure Asian cities," the ecological validity of the cues is lower than expected, producing apparent overconfidence.

The paper demonstrated that the hard-easy effect has a substantial statistical component: when items are sampled representatively from a person's reference class (using a representative sampling design), overconfidence is markedly reduced. It does not disappear entirely — there are genuine calibration failures — but the magnitude is smaller and the pattern more nuanced than the original "people are massively overconfident" narrative.

The paper spawned a productive debate between the "heuristics and biases" camp (Kahneman, Tversky, Fischhoff) and the "ecological rationality" camp (Gigerenzer) that shaped the field's understanding of when and why calibration failures occur.

Linguistic Probabilities: Variability in Verbal Probability Expressions

Wallsten, Budescu, Rapoport & Zwick · , 1986

Belief Elicitation Uncertainty Communication

Systematically measured the numeric probabilities that people assign to verbal probability expressions ("likely," "unlikely," "probable," "almost certain," etc.) across multiple experimental contexts. The key methodological contribution: asking participants to translate verbal terms into numeric probabilities and vice versa, then measuring the resulting variability.

The findings were stark. For the word "probable," numeric interpretations ranged from about 0.50 to 0.90 across participants. "Unlikely" ranged from 0.05 to 0.40. Even "certain" had a range (0.85 to 1.00). The variability was not random — each individual was relatively consistent in their own mappings — but the between-person variability was enormous. Two people who both say "likely" about the same event may hold beliefs that differ by 35 percentage points.

Context also mattered: the same word carried different numeric connotations in different domains. "Likely" for a weather forecast, a medical prognosis, and an intelligence assessment evoked different probability ranges, even within the same individual. The base rate of the event shifted interpretations: "likely" for a rare event (cancer) was assigned a lower probability than "likely" for a common event (cold).

Budescu & Wallsten (1995) extended this work, confirming the patterns across larger samples and additional languages. The practical implication is direct: any system that communicates uncertainty verbally (including LLMs, which communicate almost exclusively in natural language) introduces an unavoidable ambiguity. Two users receiving the same verbal hedge interpret it as different probabilities. Budescu et al. (2014) later showed that hybrid verbal-numeric formats ("very likely [>90%]") substantially reduce this variability.

Communicating Uncertainty in Official Economic Statistics

Manski · Annual Review of Economics, 2015

Uncertainty Communication

The specific contribution

Argues that official statistical agencies systematically suppress uncertainty by reporting point estimates when they should report interval or distributional estimates. The GDP growth rate, unemployment rate, and inflation figures that drive major policy decisions are all subject to substantial uncertainty that is rarely communicated to users. False precision — presenting an uncertain estimate as if it were known — misrepresents epistemic state and can distort downstream decisions.

The point-estimate problem

When official statistics report "GDP grew 2.1% last quarter," the reported figure typically has a standard error of 0.5–0.8 percentage points and is revised substantially in subsequent releases. Decision makers who treat the point estimate as known truth are systematically overconfident about the state of the economy. Manski argues this is an institutional failure of uncertainty communication, not merely a technical limitation — agencies could report confidence intervals but choose not to, partly because intervals appear less authoritative and are harder to headline.

The same argument applies directly to AI outputs: systems that return point predictions without uncertainty bounds are making the same institutional choice to suppress epistemic uncertainty.

Qualifications

Primarily a policy argument with economic statistics as the case study. The behavioral evidence for whether interval reporting actually improves policy decisions is thin — Manski's case rests more on normative grounds than empirical demonstration that intervals help recipients. The institutional barriers to changing official reporting formats are substantial.

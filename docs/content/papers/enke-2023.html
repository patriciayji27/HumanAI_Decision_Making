Cognitive Uncertainty

Enke & Graeber · , 2023

Decision Quality Attention & Salience

Introduced a measurable construct: cognitive uncertainty — the subjective sense of not knowing what one's own utility-maximizing choice would be. The construct is operationalized by asking participants, after making a choice, how confident they are that their choice was the right one (on a 50-100% scale, where 50% means pure guessing and 100% means certain).

The key theoretical prediction: when people are cognitively uncertain about their valuations, they compress their responses toward a cognitive default (typically the midpoint or the simpler option). This compression produces many classic behavioral anomalies — the certainty effect, the Allais paradox, stake effects, and decoy effects — as a byproduct of noise rather than stable biases. Under this account, increasing cognitive load should increase choice inconsistency, which the authors confirmed experimentally.

The model formalizes this as a Bayesian agent who has a noisy signal about their own preferences and shrinks toward a prior (the cognitive default). The degree of shrinkage depends on signal-to-noise ratio, which varies with task complexity, stakes, and the person's expertise. When stakes are high or the choice is simple, the signal is strong and behavior looks close to EU-maximizing. When stakes are low or the choice is complex, noise dominates and "biases" appear.

Tested across several economic decision tasks. The construct correlates with measured choice inconsistency (repeated identical choices) and predicts the magnitude of behavioral anomalies at the individual level. However, the empirical evidence is limited to a relatively narrow set of standard economic experiments. Whether cognitive uncertainty adds explanatory power beyond existing "noisy preferences" models (e.g., random utility) in applied settings is still open.

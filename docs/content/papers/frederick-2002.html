Time Discounting and Time Preference: A Critical Review

Frederick, Loewenstein & O'Donoghue · Journal of Economic Literature, 2002

Temporal Dynamics

The specific contribution

The definitive critical review of the intertemporal choice literature. Key finding: measured discount rates are not a stable individual trait. They vary dramatically depending on domain, stakes, gain-vs-loss framing, elicitation method, and how the delay is perceived. A person who appears to have a 10% annual discount rate in one elicitation may appear to have a 100% rate in another.

Sources of discount rate variation

Source of variationDirection of effect
Domain (health vs. money vs. time)Large — health decisions often show very high discount rates for near-term discomfort
Stakes magnitudeLarger amounts → lower measured discount rates (absolute magnitude effect)
Gains vs. lossesLosses discounted less than gains (sign effect)
Elicitation method (matching vs. choice)Matching elicits higher rates than choice procedures
Near vs. far delayNear delays → higher implicit rates (hyperbolic curvature)

The main implication: "discount rate" is not a single individual parameter that can be estimated and used to model behavior across contexts. A system that attempts to personalize to a user's discount rate based on limited behavioral data will be extrapolating across contexts in which the measured rate does not apply.

Why it matters for AI personalization

AI systems that infer time preferences from user behavior (e.g., whether users take the immediate reward in a recommendation) are likely measuring a highly context-specific preference, not a stable trait. Decisions about whether to surface long-term vs. short-term outcomes in recommendations cannot be based on a single inferred discount rate.

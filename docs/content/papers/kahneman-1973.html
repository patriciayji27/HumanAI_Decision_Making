Attention and Effort

Kahneman · Prentice-Hall, 1973

Attention & Salience

The specific contribution

Established attention as a limited cognitive resource governed by arousal and task demands. Key contributions: (1) the single-resource model — people have a general-purpose attentional pool that is allocated across competing tasks; (2) pupillary dilation as an objective measure of mental effort — pupil size tracks cognitive load in real time; (3) dual-task interference — any two tasks that both require attention will interfere, with interference proportional to the demands of each task.

Key concepts established

ConceptWhat it meansDesign implication
Attentional capacityTotal attention is finite and roughly constantAdding information has a cost — every additional display element consumes attention budget
Involuntary vs. voluntary attentionSome stimuli capture attention automatically; others require deliberate allocationSalience (size, color, motion) can hijack attention away from what users intend to process
Arousal effectsModerate arousal improves performance; too high or low degrades it (Yerkes-Dodson)High-stakes, anxiety-inducing decisions may degrade the attentional capacity needed to evaluate them well

Legacy and evolution

The single-resource model has been substantially refined — multiple-resource theories now distinguish visual, auditory, spatial, and verbal channels with partially independent capacities. But Kahneman's core insight — that attention is limited, allocation is partly involuntary, and cognitive load has real performance consequences — remains the foundation for all subsequent work on interface design, cognitive load theory, and dual-process decision models including Kahneman's own System 1 / System 2 framework in Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011).

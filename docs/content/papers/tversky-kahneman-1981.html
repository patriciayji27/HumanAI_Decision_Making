The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice

Tversky & Kahneman · Science, 1981

Decision Quality Attention & Salience

The specific contribution

Demonstrated that logically equivalent descriptions of the same choice — differing only in whether outcomes are framed as gains or losses — produce systematically different preferences. The definitive demonstration is the Asian disease problem:

FrameOption AOption BResult
Gain frame ("saves lives")Save 200 certain1/3 chance save 600, 2/3 chance save none72% chose A (risk averse)
Loss frame ("deaths")400 will die certain1/3 chance none die, 2/3 chance 600 die78% chose B (risk seeking)

Options A and B in the two frames are mathematically identical in expected deaths. The reversal (risk aversion for gains, risk seeking for losses) is predicted by prospect theory's S-shaped value function: concave above the reference point, convex below.

Why it matters for AI systems

Any AI that frames recommendations in gain or loss terms — "this treatment saves 80%" vs. "this treatment has a 20% failure rate" — can predictably shift user choices without changing the underlying evidence. This is not a bug to eliminate; it is a fundamental property of human cognition that must be accounted for in interface design. Framing is not neutral, and the choice of frame in AI output is a design decision with predictable behavioral consequences.

Qualifications

Hypothetical outcomes in survey format. Framing effects attenuate when decision makers are sophisticated, when the problem is repeated with feedback, or when both frames are shown simultaneously. Effect sizes vary across cultures and numeracy levels.

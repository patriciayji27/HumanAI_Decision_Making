Ambiguity and Comparative Ignorance

Fox & Tversky · , 1995

Risk & Ambiguity

Extended Ellsberg's (1961) ambiguity aversion findings with a comparative ignorance mechanism: ambiguity aversion is not just about unknown probabilities — it is about feeling less knowledgeable relative to the source of uncertainty or relative to a comparison standard.

In the key experiment, participants could bet on the outcome of an uncertain event. When they were offered bets on a topic they felt knowledgeable about (e.g., an NBA fan betting on basketball game outcomes), they were willing to bet at higher rates than when offered bets with equivalent probabilities on a topic they felt ignorant about (e.g., the same NBA fan betting on stock price movements). The probabilities were equated; only the participant's subjective sense of competence relative to the domain differed.

Critically, ambiguity aversion was strongest when participants could compare the ambiguous bet with a less ambiguous one. When ambiguous bets were evaluated in isolation (without a known-risk alternative), ambiguity aversion was weaker or absent. This suggests ambiguity aversion is partly a comparison effect rather than an intrinsic response to unknown probabilities.

The source-preference finding predicts that AI trust should vary by domain-relative competence: users will trust an AI system more in domains where they perceive the AI as more knowledgeable than themselves, and less in domains where they feel more competent than the AI. This predicts, for example, that a novice investor may trust an AI financial advisor more than an expert investor would — because the novice perceives greater AI competence relative to their own.

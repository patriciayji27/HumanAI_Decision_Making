More Forecasts, More (Decision) Problems: How Uncertainty Representations for Multiple Forecasts Impact Decision-Making

Sarma, Hedayati & Kay · CHI 2025

Uncertainty Communication Decision Quality

The specific contribution

Three pre-registered experiments (N≈400 total across Experiments 1–3, recruited via Prolific) testing whether the visual format used to represent multiple forecasts — ensemble CDFs vs. probability boxes (p-boxes) — changes the decision strategy participants adopt in an incentivized binary choice task. The decision task used a payoff matrix (send blankets to prevent alpaca deaths at cost $1,000/night vs. $5,000 penalty for inaction) whose optimal crossover point is Pr(freeze) ≥ 0.2.

The key framing: when there are multiple forecasts from different models, a decision-maker faces two types of uncertainty simultaneously — probabilistic uncertainty (variance within any single forecast) and incertitude (uncertainty about which forecast is correct). P-boxes suppress individual forecast information; ensembles surface it. The study asks whether suppression changes behavior.

The γ parameter is an optimism index estimated via Bayesian hierarchical linear-in-logit regression: γ=0 means the participant used only the worst-case forecast; γ=0.5 means averaging; γ=1 means best-case. Both representations produced γ≈0.25 in Experiment 1 — substantially below 0.5 (the averaging strategy prior work assumed), indicating default uncertainty aversion, not indifference.

Estimated crossover point for the average participant: Pr(freeze) ≈ 0.31, vs. the utility-optimal 0.20. Participants were consistently more risk-averse than the incentive structure warranted — most likely because they misidentified the optimal crossover point, not because of the display format.

The ensemble skew finding

In Experiment 2, when 19 of 20 ensemble forecasts clustered near the lower bound (skewed right), ~78.9% of participants had γ < 0.2 — they used essentially only the lower bound. When the cluster was near the upper bound (skewed left), 39.4% had γ > 0.8. Participants used the visual majority, not the worst case, as their reference point when the majority was visually salient. This means ensemble representations are sensitive to how the forecasts are distributed in a way that p-boxes are not (since p-boxes only show bounds).

Design implication: if a designer wants behavior unaffected by the frequency distribution of underlying model outputs, p-boxes are more reliable. If a designer wants to emphasize the central tendency, a clustered ensemble can effectively anchor users there.

Qualifications

Binary decision task only; no multi-alternative or continuous choice. Single domain (temperature/alpaca scenario). Estimated crossover-point bias and uncertainty aversion may reflect task-specific calibration effects rather than a stable display preference. Online Prolific sample; ecological validity of incentivized bonus structure for real high-stakes decisions is unknown. The study tests display × strategy alignment, not display × ultimate decision quality.

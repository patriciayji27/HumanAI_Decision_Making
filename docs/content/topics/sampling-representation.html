Sampling, Granularity, Tails & Uncertainty Representation

People who learn about a risk by reading "5% chance of failure" treat it very differently from people who learn the same risk by experiencing 1 failure in 20 trials. The description-experience gap is one of the most robust findings in judgment research — and it has troubling implications for AI that explains risk via text.

What the field is confident about

The description-experience gap is robust: described probabilities and experienced probabilities produce different behavior.

Hertwig, Barron, Weber & Erev (2004) established that people who learn about a gamble from description overweight rare events (consistent with prospect theory's probability weighting), while people who learn from sequential sampling underweight rare events. The gap is large and has been replicated across dozens of studies. The mechanism: with small samples, rare events are often not encountered at all, producing behavior that looks like underweighting.

Sample size matters enormously, and people don't sample enough.

When people learn from experience, they typically take too few samples to encounter rare events reliably. This isn't irrational — sampling is costly — but it means experience-based judgments systematically underrepresent tails. For decisions where tails matter (safety, catastrophic risk), experience-based learning is dangerous without supplementary described information.

Granularity of outcome information affects risk perception.

Showing the full distribution of outcomes (not just the expected value) changes behavior. Goldstein, Johnson & Sharpe (2008) found that showing retirement outcome distributions made people more conservative. The granularity at which uncertainty is presented — single number, interval, histogram, full distribution — is a design variable with causal effects on the decisions people make.

Where the field is still confused

Whether the description-experience gap is about sampling error or a genuine processing difference.

Fox & Hadar (2006) argued that much of the gap disappears when you control for the information people actually received (their sample). If people just happened not to encounter the rare event, they're not underweighting it — they're responding rationally to their experience. But other work (Camilleri & Newell, 2011) shows the gap persists even when controlling for information — suggesting a genuine processing mode difference, not just sampling noise.

How to combine description and experience optimally.

In the real world, people often have both described statistics and personal experience. How should these be integrated? Description corrects the small-sample problem. Experience provides vivid, memorable instances. The evidence suggests people overweight experience relative to description, even when the description is more reliable — but the optimal combination rule depends on the decision context.

What This Means for Human-AI Systems

LLMs communicate by description — which means they trigger description-mode processing. When an AI says "there's a 3% chance of X," the user is in description mode: overweighting the rare event. When the same user has experienced the AI being wrong 3 times out of 100, they're in experience mode: possibly underweighting the error rate. Designers should be aware of which mode the user is in and choose uncertainty communication strategies accordingly.

Experience-based interfaces (like HOPs) leverage sampling-mode processing deliberately. Hypothetical outcome plots, quantile dotplots, and interactive simulations convert described uncertainty into experienced uncertainty. The evidence from Stream 2 suggests this improves probability estimation for many tasks — but it also inherits the underweighting-of-rare-events problem. For high-consequence rare events, pure experience-based displays may need a described supplement.

My Take

The description-experience gap is one of the few findings in this archive that has obvious, immediate, and underexploited implications for AI system design. Most AI systems are pure description: they tell you a number or a verbal probability. But the evidence says people process experienced information differently — and sometimes better, sometimes worse. The right design probably combines both: described probability (to ensure rare events are represented) plus experiential sampling (to make the probability viscerally real). HOPs do this. Quantile dotplots approximate it. More AI systems should use these formats — and the fact that they don't is, again, a design failure, not a knowledge gap.

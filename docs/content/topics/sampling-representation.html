Sampling, Granularity, Tails & Uncertainty Representation

People who read '5% chance' and people who experience 1 failure in 20 trials process the same information differently. Description overweights rare events; experience underweights them.

Assessment

Hertwig, Barron, Weber & Erev (2004) established the description-experience gap: when probabilities are stated ("10% chance of winning 32"), people overweight rare events consistent with prospect theory. When probabilities are learned from sequential sampling, people underweight rare events — reversing the modal choice in 4 of 6 tested problems. The median sample size was 15 draws per option; with 15 draws, there is roughly a 21% probability of never encountering a 10% event at all.

Fox & Hadar (2006) argued that much of the gap is attributable to sampling error — people in the experience condition often did not encounter the rare event, so their behavior reflects rational response to incomplete information rather than a different processing mode. A residual gap persists after controlling for sample information (Camilleri & Newell, 2011), but it is smaller than the original effect. The mechanism debate (sampling error vs. processing mode) remains unresolved.

Key claims

strong canonical Described and experienced probabilities produce opposite rare-event biases.
Support
Hertwig, Barron, Weber & Erev (2004). Replicated across dozens of studies.
Qualification
Fox & Hadar (2006) showed much of the gap is sampling error. Camilleri & Newell (2011) found a residual gap, but smaller.
Verdict: Robust as a behavioral phenomenon. The causal mechanism is debated. For design, experience-based interfaces underrepresent rare events and need supplementary described information for safety-critical applications.
strong canonical Distributional granularity causally affects decisions.
Support
Goldstein, Johnson & Sharpe (2008): showing retirement distributions changed investment behavior.
Qualification
More granular displays are more cognitively demanding. For low-numeracy users, full distributions may overwhelm without improving decisions.
Verdict: Granularity is a design variable. More is not always better — it depends on the user's ability to process it.

Design implications grounded in evidence

Users in description mode overweight rare AI failures; users in experience mode underweight them. Hertwig et al. (2004) found that described rare events (p ≤ 0.10) were overweighted while experienced rare events were underweighted — flipping the modal choice in 4 of 6 tested problems. For AI systems: a new user reading "this system fails 3% of the time" is in description mode and may overweight the failure risk. A long-term user who has experienced the system working correctly 97 times out of 100 is in experience mode and may underweight the same risk — particularly if the failures they haven't personally experienced are catastrophic.

The experience condition produces small samples that miss rare events entirely. Hertwig et al.'s participants drew a median of 15 samples per option. With 15 samples, a 10% event occurs 0-1 times with ~50% probability. For AI systems with rare but consequential failure modes (e.g., 1% catastrophic error rate), experience-based trust formation is structurally unreliable — the user needs hundreds of interactions before they have enough personal experience to estimate the failure rate. Supplementary described statistics ("this system has failed 12 times in the past 1,200 cases") provide the missing base rate information.

Showing full outcome distributions changes behavior. Goldstein, Johnson & Sharpe (2008) found that showing the distribution of retirement outcomes (not just expected returns) made investors more conservative. For AI-assisted decisions, showing the distribution of possible outcomes — not just the point prediction — provides users with tail risk information that point estimates suppress.

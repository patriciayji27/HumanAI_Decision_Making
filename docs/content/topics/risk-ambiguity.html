Risk, Ambiguity & Controllability

Risk (known probabilities) and ambiguity (unknown probabilities) are processed differently. AI systems are almost always sources of ambiguity — users do not know the distribution over AI errors.

Assessment

Ellsberg (1961) demonstrated ambiguity aversion using two thought experiments. In the classic version: an urn with 90 balls — 30 red, 60 either black or yellow in unknown proportions. Most people prefer to bet on the known proportion (red, 1/3) over the unknown (black, could be 0/90 or 60/90), even though by any subjective probability assignment, one of the unknown-proportion bets should be at least as attractive. This violates the sure-thing principle of expected utility theory.

Ambiguity attitudes are not uniform across contexts. Fox & Tversky (1995) showed that willingness to bet under ambiguity depends on perceived competence relative to the uncertainty source — an NBA fan is more willing to bet on basketball outcomes than on equivalent-probability stock movements. Ambiguity aversion also varies with probability level: people tend to be ambiguity-averse for likely gains but can be ambiguity-seeking for unlikely gains.

AI systems typically present ambiguous uncertainty — users do not know the distribution of the AI's errors across cases. Whether this triggers standard ambiguity aversion (treatable by providing track record data that converts ambiguity to risk) or a distinct response tied to process opacity is an open question with limited direct evidence.

Key claims

strong canonical Ambiguity aversion is robust — people pay a premium to avoid unknown probabilities.
Support
Ellsberg (1961). Replicated extensively.
Qualification
Attitudes vary by source competence (Fox & Tversky, 1995), domain, probability level, and individual.
Verdict: Robust as a central tendency. The heterogeneity matters for design — do not assume all users respond to AI uncertainty the same way.
strong canonical Perceived control modulates risk-taking, even when the control is illusory.
Support
Langer (1975). Giving people choice, familiarity, or involvement cues makes them behave as if they can influence random outcomes.
Qualification
For AI design, the effect is double-edged: user-adjustable parameters may increase comfort (beneficial) but also create false confidence (harmful).
Verdict: Provide genuine control where possible. Be transparent about the limits of user adjustments.

Design implications grounded in evidence

Providing domain-specific track records converts ambiguity to risk. Fox & Tversky (1995) showed willingness to act under uncertainty increases when the person perceives themselves as knowledgeable relative to the uncertainty source. For AI systems, showing "this model's accuracy on cases similar to yours is 84% (based on 12,000 validation cases)" converts an ambiguous AI output into a risk assessment — and ambiguity aversion predicts this should increase appropriate reliance. Generic accuracy claims ("this model is 90% accurate overall") are less effective because they don't address the user's specific uncertainty about their case.

Illusory control increases comfort but may not improve decisions. Langer (1975) showed that letting people choose lottery numbers or flip their own coins increased their confidence in random outcomes. For AI, the analog is letting users adjust thresholds, select features, or tune parameters. Dietvorst et al. (2015, Experiment 6) showed this works — usage jumped from 36% to 72% when users could modify the output by up to 10 points. But the user's confidence in the modified output may exceed its actual reliability, particularly if the modification is cosmetic.

Temporal Dynamics: Myopia, Feedback & Time Horizon

How often you check changes how much risk you take. Myopic loss aversion — loss aversion interacting with evaluation frequency — has the cleanest design implications in this archive.

Assessment

Benartzi & Thaler (1995) showed that a loss-averse investor (λ≈2) evaluating their portfolio annually is roughly indifferent between the historical stock and bond return distributions. With more frequent evaluation, losses become more visible — stocks lose value in roughly 40% of months but only 30% of years — and the loss-averse investor demands a larger premium to hold stocks. This interaction of evaluation frequency with loss aversion can account for the historically observed equity premium (~6%).

Gneezy & Potters (1997) confirmed this experimentally: subjects who saw outcomes every round invested 50.8% of their endowment in a risky option; those who saw outcomes every three rounds invested 67.3% — a 33% increase from a pure information-timing manipulation. Haigh & List (2005) found professional CBOT traders show the same pattern, indicating that financial expertise does not eliminate the effect.

Key claims

strong canonical Frequent evaluation reduces risk-taking because it increases the probability of observing losses.
Support
Benartzi & Thaler (1995), Gneezy & Potters (1997): subjects seeing outcomes every period invested ~40% less in risky options. Haigh & List (2005): professional traders are also susceptible.
Qualification
Most evidence is from financial contexts. Generalization to non-financial repeated decisions is assumed but not demonstrated.
Verdict: Among the most cleanly established and actionable findings in behavioral decision research. Evaluation frequency is a design variable that should be chosen deliberately.

Key papers

  • strong canonical Benartzi & Thaler (1995) Myopic loss aversion — foundational
  • strong canonical Gneezy & Potters (1997) Experimental confirmation
  • mixed debated Haigh & List (2005) Professionals are also susceptible

Design implications grounded in evidence

Reducing evaluation frequency from every round to every 3 rounds increased risky investment by ~40%. Gneezy & Potters (1997) demonstrated this experimentally. The mechanism: less frequent evaluation reduces the probability of seeing a loss in any given evaluation period, and loss-averse agents respond to loss probability, not just expected return. For AI dashboards showing model performance, switching from daily to weekly or monthly reporting should reduce loss-averse reactions to normal performance variation — but only if the longer interval still provides sufficient signal for detecting genuine problems.

Professional traders show the same effect. Haigh & List (2005) found myopic loss aversion among CBOT professional traders, ruling out the hypothesis that expertise or experience eliminates the bias. If professional traders are susceptible, users of AI systems — who typically have less practice with probabilistic feedback — should be assumed susceptible as well.

Broad framing (portfolio-level reporting) reduces loss aversion's impact. Thaler et al. (1997) showed that presenting multiple gambles as a package rather than individually increased risk-taking. For AI systems, this translates to: show "your portfolio is up 12% this year" rather than individual-stock movements; show "the model correctly classified 94% of this month's cases" rather than highlighting each daily error.

AI Agents as Decision Partners

Calibrated AI confidence is necessary but not sufficient for good human-AI decisions. Explanation style, decision protocol, and the user's ability to detect AI errors matter at least as much — and possibly more.

Assessment

Correct AI explanations improve human decision accuracy by roughly 5 percentage points in studied tasks (Schemmer et al., 2025), while incorrect explanations reduce it by roughly 15 points — a 3:1 asymmetry that changes the expected value calculation for shipping explanation features. This asymmetry has been demonstrated in controlled experiments but not yet broadly replicated across domains.

Human-AI teams outperform both human alone and AI alone in a minority of studied conditions (Vaccaro et al., 2024 meta-analysis of 106 studies). The main moderators are AI accuracy (moderate works best) and task difficulty. The default outcome in most studied settings is that the human's involvement adds noise rather than value, primarily because users accept AI predictions at ~80% rates regardless of correctness (Bansal et al., 2021).

Protocol-level interventions (cognitive forcing, structured disagreement) have produced larger reductions in overreliance than explanation-level interventions (XAI features) in the studies that have compared them directly. Buçinca et al. (2021) found that requiring users to commit before seeing AI output reduced agreement with incorrect AI from 69% to 52%, while adding LIME explanations had no detectable effect.

Key claims

strong canonical Algorithm aversion and algorithm appreciation are both real — the direction is context-sensitive, not a stable individual trait.
Support
Dietvorst et al. (2015) demonstrated aversion robustly. Logg, Minson & Moore (2019) showed the reverse for objective-seeming tasks. Horowitz & Kahn (2024) found a Dunning-Kruger pattern: low AI knowledge → aversion, medium → overreliance, high → calibrated.
Qualification
The context-dependence structure (task type × failure visibility × user expertise × perceived AI competence) is still being mapped. Current models don't predict which regime a given setting will fall into.
Verdict: The phenomena are real. Treat this as a calibration problem: most users arrive with miscalibrated priors about AI reliability, and the challenge is correcting those priors.
strong canonical Human-AI complementarity is achieved in a minority of studied conditions.
Support
Vaccaro, Almaatouq & Malone (2024) meta-analysis found complementarity is not the default. Bansal et al. (2021) showed it requires humans to identify when the AI is wrong — which is precisely the task humans perform worst at.
Qualification
Most studies use classification tasks with known ground truth. In domains where humans have strong expertise and AI errors are systematically different, complementarity may be more achievable. Publication bias may also understate the failure rate.
Verdict: As a default assumption, complementarity is unlikely without specific design interventions. The productive question is which protocol features increase the probability of complementarity, and at what cognitive cost.
mixed emerging Incorrect AI explanations damage decisions more than correct explanations improve them — the impact is asymmetric.
Support
Schemmer et al. (2025) demonstrated this in controlled experiments. Vasconcelos et al. (2023) found explanations reduce overreliance only when cognitive effort of engagement is low relative to stakes.
Qualification
The evidence base is small. The asymmetry has been shown in specific domains but not broadly replicated. The definition of 'incorrect explanation' varies across studies.
Verdict: Promising and important if it replicates. Every explanation feature should be evaluated by asking what happens when it is wrong, not just whether it helps when it is right.
mixed emerging LLMs are reluctant to express uncertainty, likely due to RLHF training, and non-expert users are especially susceptible to unhedged output.
Support
Steyvers & Peters (2025) reviewed metacognition in LLMs and found systematic reluctance to express uncertainty. Zhou et al. (2024) documented how RLHF stylistically suppresses hedging.
Qualification
Primarily a theoretical framework with predictions laid out but not yet tested. The magnitude of user susceptibility to false fluency in naturalistic settings is unknown.
Verdict: The theoretical case is strong and the mechanism is plausible. LLM output formatting is a tractable intervention point — the question is whether AI labs will prioritize calibrated hedging over fluent confidence.

Design implications grounded in evidence

Allowing users to modify AI output restores adoption without sacrificing accuracy. Dietvorst et al. (2015, Experiment 6) found that letting participants adjust the algorithm's forecast by up to 10 points raised usage from 36% to 72% — with negligible impact on forecast quality. The practical implementation: editable confidence scores, adjustable parameters, or "AI draft + user edit" workflows rather than accept/reject interfaces.

Cognitive forcing (committing to a judgment before seeing AI output) reduced agreement with incorrect AI predictions from 69% to 52%. Buçinca et al. (2021, Study 1) demonstrated this across three experiments in a calorie estimation task. The cost: ~50% more time per decision and lower reported satisfaction. This makes cognitive forcing practical for high-stakes, low-frequency decisions (medical diagnosis, loan approval) but impractical for routine automation (content moderation, spam filtering).

Explanations reduce overreliance only when evaluation cost is low relative to stakes. Vasconcelos et al. (2023) formalized this as a cost-benefit function across five studies: when explanation evaluation required minimal effort, overreliance dropped 12-20 percentage points. When effort was high, no reduction occurred. Design implication: short, verifiable explanations (single most relevant feature, binary comparison) will outperform comprehensive explanations (full feature attribution, multi-paragraph rationale) in most production settings.

Incorrect explanations hurt ~3× as much as correct ones help. Schemmer et al. (2025) found that correct explanations improved accuracy by ~5 percentage points while incorrect explanations decreased it by ~15 points. If this ratio holds broadly, explanation features have negative expected value whenever explanation accuracy falls below ~75%. This means explanation accuracy should be a shipping criterion, not just model accuracy.

Human-AI complementarity is achieved in a minority of studied conditions. Vaccaro et al. (2024, meta-analysis of 106 studies) found that the modal outcome is AI alone performing comparably to the human-AI team. Complementarity requires moderate AI accuracy (high enough to be useful, low enough that human override adds value), sufficient task difficulty, and expert human overseers. Before investing in human-in-the-loop architectures, assess whether these conditions are met.

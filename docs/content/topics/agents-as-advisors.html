AI Agents as Decision Partners

How AI recommendations actually affect human decisions — and why the answer is almost never just about whether the AI is right.

What the evidence shows

Human-AI teams outperform either side alone in a minority of studied conditions — Vaccaro et al.'s 2024 meta-analysis of 106 studies found this is not the default outcome. The modal result is that AI alone performs comparably to the human-AI team, because humans accept AI predictions at roughly 80% regardless of correctness (Bansal et al., 2021).

The most tractable lever is not explanation quality but protocol design. Buçinca et al. (2021) found that requiring users to commit to a judgment before seeing AI output reduced agreement with incorrect AI from 69% to 52% — a larger effect than adding explanation features. Schemmer et al. (2025) found incorrect explanations hurt decision quality ~3× more than correct ones help, which means explanation accuracy needs to be a shipping criterion alongside model accuracy.

Reliance priors: aversion, appreciation, and trust calibration

Before users interact with an AI system, they arrive with a prior about whether to trust it. That prior is neither stable nor rational — it depends on task type, visible failures, perceived AI competence relative to their own, and how much they know about AI in general.

Algorithm aversion (Dietvorst et al., 2015) Seeing an algorithm fail — even when it still outperforms humans on average — causes users to abandon it at rates well below what expected-value reasoning would recommend. The aversion is triggered by failure salience, not objective performance. Letting users modify the algorithm's output (by as little as ±10 units) largely eliminated the aversion without hurting accuracy.
Algorithm appreciation (Logg et al., 2019) For tasks perceived as objective or numerical, users often over-weight algorithm recommendations. The direction of bias (aversion vs. appreciation) depends on perceived task subjectivity — it is not a stable individual trait.
AI knowledge follows a Dunning-Kruger pattern (Horowitz & Kahn, 2024) Across a 9-country experiment (N=9,000), users with low AI background knowledge showed aversion; medium-knowledge users showed the strongest overreliance; high-knowledge users were best calibrated. The implication: the most dangerous reliance profile is partial knowledge, not ignorance.
Design takeaway
Treat reliance as a calibration problem, not a binary setting. Users with partial AI knowledge need the most active management — they are confident but wrong about when to trust the system. Editable interfaces (allow modification of AI output) reduce aversion without degrading performance.
Protocol design: when advice arrives and how users are asked to respond

The protocol — the structural rules governing how AI advice is presented and when — is arguably the highest-leverage intervention point. Small changes to sequencing produce large changes in reliance behavior.

For routine decisions, AI-flags-disagreement is a lower-cost alternative, though anchoring effects reduce its impact.
Explanation economics: when explanations help, harm, or cost too much

XAI features are often shipped on the assumption that transparency improves outcomes. The evidence is less optimistic. Explanations have a cost-benefit structure that depends critically on explanation accuracy, user cognitive budget, and stakes.

Asymmetric damage (Schemmer et al., 2025) Correct explanations improved accuracy by ~5 percentage points. Incorrect explanations decreased it by ~15 points — a 3:1 asymmetry. If this ratio holds broadly, explanation features have negative expected value whenever explanation accuracy falls below ~75%.
Cost-benefit threshold (Vasconcelos et al., 2023) Explanations reduced overreliance only when the cognitive cost of evaluating them was low relative to decision stakes. When effort was high, overreliance persisted regardless of explanation quality. Across five studies, overreliance dropped 12–20 percentage points when effort was low.
Verifiability is the key (Fok & Weld, 2023) Most current explanation designs do not produce explanations users can actually verify — they produce explanations that look authoritative. Verifiable explanations (single most-relevant feature, binary comparison) are the exception, not the rule.
Design takeaway
Before shipping an explanation feature, ask: what happens when the explanation is wrong? Explanation accuracy should be a shipping criterion alongside model accuracy. Short, verifiable explanations (one feature, binary format) outperform comprehensive explanations in most production settings. Withhold explanations when model confidence is low.
Uncertainty expression: confidence, hedging, and false fluency

AI systems communicate — or fail to communicate — their own uncertainty through linguistic choices, confidence scores, and hedging behavior. These choices have measurable downstream effects on user reliance.

LLMs suppress uncertainty (Steyvers & Peters, 2025) LLMs trained with RLHF are stylistically incentivized toward confident, fluent output. Users — especially non-experts — cannot reliably distinguish AI confidence from AI accuracy. The result: overreliance on wrong-but-fluent output.
Visual uncertainty quantification improves calibration (Prabhudesai et al., 2023) Displaying AI uncertainty visually (rather than numerically) improved human accuracy and reduced overreliance on high-confidence errors in an image classification task. The benefit was strongest when uncertainty was explicitly tied to the specific decision, not shown as a global model score.
Uncertainty-aware delegation (Lee et al., 2025) Distance-based uncertainty scores (tied to how far a case is from the training distribution) outperformed probability-based confidence scores for routing decisions between human and AI, yielding 8.2% higher correct decisions in a stroke rehabilitation domain.
Design takeaway
Decouple confidence signals from prose style. If LLM output sounds confident, that is a stylistic artifact, not a calibration guarantee. Case-specific uncertainty displays outperform global model confidence scores. Hedging language ("I'm less certain about this") in LLM output is technically feasible — it requires prompt engineering, not model changes.
Complementarity conditions: when the team can actually beat both sides

The standard framing of human-AI collaboration assumes that combining human and AI judgment produces better outcomes than either alone. The conditions under which this is actually true are more specific than the field has assumed.

Complementarity requires error independence (Steyvers et al., 2022) The Bayesian model of human-AI integration predicts complementarity only when human and AI errors are uncorrelated and both are well-calibrated. In practice, both conditions are regularly violated: AI errors are systematic and humans anchor on AI output, creating correlated errors.
The meta-analytic baseline (Vaccaro et al., 2024) Across 106 studies, complementarity was achieved in a minority of conditions. Task difficulty and AI accuracy were the main moderators: AI accuracy at ~80% and task difficulty that challenges humans but not AI was the strongest predictor. When AI accuracy is very high or very low, human involvement adds noise rather than value.
The detection problem (Bansal et al., 2021) Complementarity requires humans to identify when AI is wrong. This is precisely the task humans perform worst at in studied contexts: users accept AI predictions at ~80% regardless of AI accuracy. The hardest complementarity condition to meet is the most critical one.
Design takeaway
Before investing in human-in-the-loop architectures, verify whether complementarity conditions are met: is AI accuracy in the 70–85% range? Are human and AI errors genuinely uncorrelated? Do users have enough domain expertise to detect AI failures? If these conditions are not met, the human adds noise rather than judgment, and a cleaner human-out-of-the-loop design may outperform.

Belief Elicitation & Calibration

Asking someone about their beliefs changes those beliefs. The method used to ask is not a neutral channel — and there may be no single ground-truth internal probability to recover in the first place.

What the evidence shows

The overconfidence finding — 90% confidence intervals contain the true value about 50% of the time — is among the most replicated in judgment research (Lichtenstein, Fischhoff & Phillips, 1982). The mechanism is anchoring-and-adjustment: people anchor on a central estimate and spread too narrowly. But the ecological rationality critique (Gigerenzer et al., 1991) showed the magnitude depends on item selection: using hard, unrepresentative questions inflates apparent miscalibration.

Elicited beliefs depend on format. The same person, asked the same question via numeric scale, probability wheel, verbal expression, or distribution builder, produces systematically different estimates (Wallsten et al., 1986). This is not noise — it is evidence that elicitation is partly constructive. The method shapes the belief it claims to measure.

The central insight of this stream

There may be no single ground-truth internal probability to recover.

This is a stronger claim than "measurement is imperfect." It suggests that for many beliefs, the belief itself is not a stable, pre-formed object that elicitation merely reads off. The belief is partly constructed in the act of response. Different elicitation formats access different representations — or generate different representations — rather than measuring the same underlying thing through different instruments. The practical implication: no single elicitation format is the "correct" one, and format choice is a substantive design decision, not a cosmetic one.

Three distinct problems that get conflated

Elicitation as measurement
The goal is to accurately read out a belief that already exists — minimizing the distortion introduced by the elicitation instrument.
Forecasting tournaments. Proper scoring rules. Confidence calibration tasks. Research question: how well does the method recover the "true" belief?
Elicitation as training
The goal is to improve the quality of future beliefs through the act of elicitation — using the process to develop calibration skills.
The Good Judgment Project. Calibration training programs. AI tutoring systems. Research question: does regular elicitation + feedback improve subsequent forecasting?
Elicitation as interface scaffolding
The goal is to design an interaction where users specify their uncertainty in a way that helps them make better downstream decisions — regardless of what the "true" belief is.
Distribution builders for retirement planning. AI systems that ask about confidence before giving a recommendation. Research question: which elicitation design produces better decisions?

These three goals conflict. What is optimal for measurement (proper scoring rules) may not be optimal for training (adaptive difficulty, feedback timing), which may not be optimal for interface scaffolding (low cognitive load, decision-relevant outputs).

Four types of beliefs worth distinguishing

The constructive view of belief elicitation implies that different beliefs exist in different relationships to the elicitation act. These categories matter for AI system design.

Type 1
Beliefs already held
Pre-formed prior beliefs that the person holds before any elicitation. Elicitation attempts to read these out without disturbing them. Example: "what do you think the probability of rain is tomorrow?" when the person has already checked a forecast.
Type 2
Beliefs constructed in response
Beliefs generated on the spot in response to an unexpected question. The person had no prior, and the belief is constructed from available cues during the elicitation itself. These are especially sensitive to anchors and framing.
Type 3
Beliefs stabilized through feedback
Beliefs that begin as noisy or uncertain but become more stable through repeated elicitation with feedback. Superforecasters' probability estimates have this character — the high calibration is an outcome of the process, not a pre-existing trait.
Type 4
Beliefs transformed by AI interaction
Beliefs that are reshaped by interacting with an AI elicitor — which may confirm, challenge, or restructure the person's prior. An AI that explains its own uncertainty while asking about yours is not just measuring; it is intervening. The measurement and training problems collapse into one.

Key claims

strong canonical Confidence intervals are systematically overconfident — 90% intervals contain truth about 50% of the time.
Support
Lichtenstein, Fischhoff & Phillips (1982). Soll & Klayman (2004) identified anchoring-and-adjustment as the mechanism.
Qualification
Magnitude depends on item difficulty and selection. Ecological sampling (representative items) substantially reduces apparent miscalibration. Frequency-estimation tasks show less overconfidence than confidence-interval tasks.
Verdict: The directional finding is robust. But "people are overconfident" is a family of context-dependent phenomena, not a single uniform bias — and each variant has a different mechanism and different intervention.
strong canonical Elicitation format causally affects the belief measured.
Support
The same person produces different estimates on numeric scales, probability wheels, distribution builders, and verbal expressions. Wallsten et al. (1986) documented that "likely" spans 0.55 to 0.90 across individuals.
Qualification
Format sensitivity does not mean elicitation is useless — it means format is a design variable. Within a given format, individual differences often exceed format differences.
Verdict: Format is not neutral. Any system that elicits beliefs without reporting and justifying the format choice is incomplete as a scientific instrument and as a design artifact.
strong canonical Calibration is a trainable skill with stable individual differences.
Support
Tetlock's Good Judgment Project (Mellers et al., 2015). Decomposition, base-rate thinking, and incremental updating characterize the best performers — and can be taught.
Qualification
Trainability is domain-specific. Cross-domain transfer is weak. Domains with sparse or delayed feedback show less improvement. "Superforecaster" performance may partly reflect selection effects.
Verdict: AI-delivered calibration training is a high-value application with a real evidence base. The constraint is that training must be domain-specific — expecting generalization is not supported.

  • strong canonical Soll & Klayman (2004) Anchoring mechanism for narrow intervals
  • strong canonical Mellers et al. (2015) / Tetlock (2015) Superforecasting — calibration as trainable skill
  • strong debated Gigerenzer, Hoffrage & Kleinbölting (1991) Ecological critique — item selection inflates overconfidence
  • strong canonical Gneiting & Raftery (2007) Unified proper scoring rule theory — with known limitations under CPT
  • mixed debated Goldstein & Rothschild (2014) Distribution builders for non-experts — promising but load-sensitive

Design implications grounded in evidence

Know which elicitation problem you are solving before picking a format. A forecasting tournament needs measurement-optimized elicitation (proper scoring rules, high granularity). A calibration training tool needs training-optimized elicitation (adaptive difficulty, immediate feedback). An AI decision interface needs interface-optimized elicitation (low cognitive load, decision-relevant output). These have different optimal formats.

Verbal elicitation in LLM interfaces produces the widest ambiguity. When users say "I think it's likely" and the AI responds "I also think it's likely," the two parties may hold beliefs 35 percentage points apart (Wallsten et al., 1986). Hybrid verbal-numeric formats reduced IPCC misinterpretation from ~40% to ~15% (Budescu et al., 2014) — the same logic applies to AI conversational interfaces. Adding numeric anchors to verbal probability terms is a low-cost, high-impact improvement.

Calibration training via AI is feasible and well-motivated. The Good Judgment Project demonstrated that decomposition, base-rate thinking, and incremental updating raise forecasting performance substantially when taught systematically. AI tutoring with adaptive difficulty and domain-specific question banks could deliver this at scale. The binding constraint is domain specificity — the training effect does not transfer broadly, so the system needs to train in the relevant domain.

Belief Elicitation & Calibration

The method used to ask changes the belief you get back. Different formats access different aspects of a person's uncertainty representation, and there may be no single ground-truth internal probability to recover.

Assessment

The overconfidence finding — 90% confidence intervals contain the true value about 50% of the time — is among the most replicated in judgment research (Lichtenstein, Fischhoff & Phillips, 1982). The mechanism is anchoring-and-adjustment: people anchor on a central estimate and don't spread wide enough (Soll & Klayman, 2004). However, the ecological rationality critique (Gigerenzer et al., 1991) showed the magnitude depends substantially on how test questions are selected — using hard, unrepresentative items inflates measured overconfidence relative to ecologically sampled items.

Elicited beliefs depend on the format used to ask. The same person, asked the same question via numeric scale, probability wheel, verbal expression, or distribution builder, produces systematically different probability estimates (Wallsten et al., 1986). This suggests elicitation is partly constructive — the method shapes the belief it claims to measure — rather than purely revelatory.

Proper scoring rules (Brier, logarithmic) incentivize truthful reporting under expected utility theory, but under prospect theory's nonlinear probability weighting, the optimal report under a "proper" scoring rule may diverge from the person's actual belief. Theoretical corrections exist (Offerman et al., 2009) but are rarely implemented in practice.

Key claims

strong canonical Confidence intervals are systematically overconfident — 90% intervals contain truth about 50% of the time.
Support
Lichtenstein, Fischhoff & Phillips (1982). Soll & Klayman (2004) identified the mechanism: anchoring on a central estimate with insufficient outward adjustment.
Qualification
Magnitude depends on item difficulty and selection. The hard-easy effect is well-established. Ecological sampling reduces measured miscalibration. Frequency-estimation tasks show less overconfidence than confidence-interval tasks.
Verdict: The directional finding is among the most robust in judgment research. But 'people are overconfident' is a family of context-dependent phenomena with distinct mechanisms, not a single uniform bias.
strong canonical Elicitation format causally affects the belief measured.
Support
The same person produces different estimates on numeric scales, probability wheels, distribution builders, and verbal expressions. Wallsten et al. (1986) documented enormous variability in verbal terms.
Qualification
Format sensitivity does not mean elicitation is useless — it means format is a design variable. Within a given format, individual differences often exceed format differences.
Verdict: Format is not neutral. Any system that elicits beliefs without reporting and justifying the format choice is incomplete.
strong canonical Calibration is a trainable skill with stable individual differences.
Support
Tetlock's Good Judgment Project (Mellers et al., 2015). Decomposition, base-rate thinking, and incremental updating can be taught.
Qualification
Trainability varies by domain. Domains with sparse or delayed feedback show less improvement. 'Superforecaster' status may partly reflect selection effects. Cross-domain transfer is weak.
Verdict: Calibration training via AI tutoring is a high-value, low-risk application. But expecting generalization beyond the training domain is not supported.

Design implications grounded in evidence

Verbal elicitation (the default LLM format) produces the widest variability. Wallsten et al. (1986) documented that "likely" means 0.55 to 0.90 across individuals. For an AI system eliciting beliefs through conversation, this means two users who both say "likely" may hold beliefs differing by 35 percentage points. Hybrid verbal-numeric formats ("very likely [>90%]") reduced misinterpretation from ~40% to ~15% in IPCC communication (Budescu et al., 2014). Implementing this in LLM output requires only formatting changes, not model changes.

Calibration training can be delivered via AI at scale. The Good Judgment Project (Mellers et al., 2015) demonstrated that decomposition, base-rate thinking, and incremental updating raise forecasting performance from the median to the top decile when taught systematically. An AI tutoring system with adaptive difficulty, personalized feedback, and domain-specific question banks could deliver this training at marginal cost near zero. The constraint: domain transfer is weak (Tetlock, 2015), so training must be domain-specific.

Distribution builders capture more information than point estimates but only with trained users. Goldstein & Rothschild (2014) showed that interactive bin-filling interfaces let non-experts construct meaningful full distributions. But performance depends on bin count, default shapes, and whether the interface uses density or cumulative format. For practical deployment, a three-quantile approach (P10/P50/P90) balances information capture against cognitive load.

Belief Elicitation & Calibration

The method you use to ask changes the belief you get back. There is probably no single "true belief" sitting in memory — elicitation is partly generative, and the design challenge is minimizing distortion rather than achieving neutrality.

→ Uncertainty Communication → Decision Quality → Attention & Salience → Sampling & Experience → AI Agents

What the field is confident about

People are overconfident in confidence intervals — massively and consistently.

90% confidence intervals contain the truth about 50% of the time. This is one of the most replicated findings in judgment research (Lichtenstein, Fischhoff & Phillips, 1982). It holds across domains, populations, and expertise levels — though the magnitude varies. The mechanism is anchoring-and-adjustment: people center on a best guess and don't spread wide enough (Soll & Klayman, 2004).

Elicitation format causally affects the belief you measure.

The same person, asked the same question, produces different probability estimates depending on whether you use a numeric scale, a probability wheel, a verbal expression, or a distribution builder. This isn't noise — it's systematic. Verbal "likely" can mean anywhere from 0.55 to 0.90 across individuals (Wallsten et al., 1986). This means there may not be a single ground-truth internal probability — different methods access different aspects of the person's uncertainty representation.

Calibration is trainable, and superforecasters are real.

Tetlock's Good Judgment Project showed that a small subset of forecasters are consistently well-calibrated and well-resolved, and that their methods — decomposition, base-rate thinking, incremental updating, disconfirmation-seeking — can be taught. This means calibration is partly a skill, not just a fixed cognitive trait. But trainability varies: weather forecasters are well-calibrated (because they get frequent, clear feedback); physicians typically are not.

Where the field is still confused

How much of "overconfidence" is a real bias vs. a statistical artifact?

The ecological rationality camp (Gigerenzer et al., 1991) argues that overconfidence is partly an artifact of experimenters selecting difficult, unrepresentative questions. When questions are sampled representatively from the person's natural environment, miscalibration shrinks. The hard-easy effect is well-established (overconfidence on hard items, underconfidence on easy items), and Klayman et al. (1999) showed a substantial statistical component. This doesn't eliminate overconfidence, but it complicates the narrative.

What does probability weighting do to incentivized elicitation?

Proper scoring rules (Brier, logarithmic) incentivize truthful reporting — but only under expected utility. Under prospect theory, where probabilities are weighted nonlinearly, the optimal report under a "proper" scoring rule diverges from the true belief. Theoretical fixes exist (Offerman et al., 2009; Schlag et al., 2015) but empirical implementations are limited. This means all incentivized elicitation data should be interpreted with caution.

Is calibration a stable individual trait or a context-dependent state?

Superforecaster research says trait. Domain variation says state (the same person can be well-calibrated for frequency estimates and poorly calibrated for confidence intervals). Format variation says state. The truth is probably both — there's a stable individual-differences component plus large context effects. But the field hasn't cleanly decomposed these, which matters for whether you invest in training people or redesigning interfaces.

Canonical Paradigms & Measures

Proper scoring rules are the theoretical backbone. A scoring rule is proper if truthful reporting maximizes expected score under expected utility. The two workhorses: the Brier score (quadratic, bounded, decomposable into calibration + resolution + uncertainty) and the logarithmic score (local, unbounded, uniquely information-theoretic). Gneiting & Raftery (2007) unified the theory for continuous forecasts with the CRPS.

Calibration decomposition (Murphy, 1973): forecast quality = calibration (do stated probabilities match frequencies?) + resolution (do probabilities vary appropriately across events?) + sharpness (are probabilities spread widely, not clustered near the base rate?). A well-calibrated but unresolved forecaster is useless. A well-resolved but miscalibrated forecaster can sometimes be recalibrated post-hoc.

Incentive compatibility mechanisms: binary lottery procedures (Holt, 1986), matching probabilities (Dyer & Miles, 1998), proper scoring rules directly. All assume expected utility maximization — which means all are compromised under prospect theory. This is a fundamental tension the field hasn't resolved.

Best Papers to Start With

  • Settled Lichtenstein, Fischhoff & Phillips (1982) The overconfidence review — established the core finding
  • Settled Wallsten, Budescu, Rapoport & Zwick (1986) Verbal probability variability — still the reference
  • Settled Soll & Klayman (2004) Anchoring-and-adjustment mechanism for narrow intervals
  • Settled Tetlock (2015) / Mellers et al. (2015) Superforecasting — calibration as trainable skill
  • Contested Gigerenzer, Hoffrage & Kleinbölting (1991) Ecological challenge to overconfidence — important counterpoint
  • Contested Goldstein & Rothschild (2014) Distribution builders — full distributions are elicitable from non-experts
  • Frontier Gneiting & Raftery (2007) Unified scoring rule theory for probabilistic forecasts

What This Means for Human-AI Systems

If elicitation is partly generative, AI-mediated elicitation will shape the beliefs it claims to measure. Every design choice in a conversational elicitation interface — question order, decomposition strategy, response format, anchoring cues — is a potential distortion source. The standard for AI elicitation should not be "does the user report a number?" but "does the reported number predict their downstream behavior better than alternatives?"

Verbal probability is the default AI output format, and it's the worst one for precision. LLMs communicate in natural language. Verbal probability expressions are the most ambiguous elicitation/communication format we know. Any AI system that expresses or elicits beliefs verbally without numeric grounding is operating in the worst part of the design space. Hybrid verbal-numeric formats (like the IPCC's "very likely [>90%]") are an immediate improvement.

Calibration training scales via AI. If superforecaster methods are teachable, AI tutoring systems could deliver calibration training at scale — with personalized feedback, adaptive difficulty, and domain-specific question banks. This is a high-value, low-risk application of AI in this space.

Open Experiment Ideas

  • Format × numeracy × AI interaction Compare elicitation formats (point, interval, distribution builder, verbal) crossed with user numeracy (assessed via BNT) crossed with AI assistance (none, static tooltips, conversational AI). Primary outcome: calibration and resolution of elicited beliefs.
  • Prospect-theory-robust scoring rules in practice Implement the theoretical scoring rules that are proper under probability weighting (Offerman et al., 2009) and test whether they actually produce less distorted elicited beliefs than standard Brier/log scoring.
  • Second-order uncertainty elicitation Design interfaces that capture not just "what do you believe?" but "how sure are you about your belief?" — meta-uncertainty. Test whether capturing second-order uncertainty improves downstream decisions (compared to collapsing everything into a wider first-order interval).
My Take

The elicitation literature has a measurement problem that it hasn't fully reckoned with: if elicitation is constructive (not merely revelatory), then "the person's true belief" may not be a well-defined target. This doesn't make elicitation useless — it makes it a design problem rather than a measurement problem. The right question is not "which method gets closest to the true belief?" but "which method produces reports that best predict the person's behavior and best support their decisions?" That shift — from accuracy-of-measurement to utility-of-measurement — reframes the entire stream and connects it directly to Stream 3 (decision quality). The field is moving this direction but slowly.

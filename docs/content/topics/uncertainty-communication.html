Uncertainty Communication

Format causally affects decisions. The same probability, presented differently, produces measurably different behavior — sometimes large enough to reverse a preference.

Assessment

Natural frequencies raise correct Bayesian inference from roughly 16% to 46% (Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, 1995), one of the largest format effects in the judgment literature. The benefit is strongest for multi-step Bayesian integration tasks and smaller for simple probability judgments (Brase, 2008). The frequencies must preserve the natural sampling structure to produce the effect.

Hofman, Goldstein & Hullman (2020) demonstrated that displays optimized for comprehension and displays optimized for decision quality can rank differently — a display that helps people accurately report a probability may not help them make a better choice based on that probability. This comprehension-use gap has been shown in specific tasks but its generality across domains is still being established.

Numeracy moderates nearly all format effects in this stream. Low-numeracy individuals (~30-50% of the general population by standard measures) are disproportionately harmed by formats requiring arithmetic but benefit most from visual aids like icon arrays (Garcia-Retamero & Cokely, 2013). High-numeracy individuals extract both more precise and more affective meaning from numbers (Peters et al., 2006).

Key claims

strong canonical Natural frequencies dramatically improve Bayesian reasoning.
Support
Gigerenzer & Hoffrage (1995): ~15% to ~50% correct inference. Replicated across dozens of studies.
Qualification
Benefit strongest for multi-step Bayesian problems. Smaller for simple judgments. Frequencies must be 'natural' (preserving reference class structure). The evolved-processor interpretation is debated.
Verdict: Robust and practically important. Interfaces requiring Bayesian reasoning should present information as natural frequencies. The broader claim — that frequency formats should be used universally — is less well-supported.
mixed debated Displays that improve comprehension can worsen decision quality.
Support
Hofman, Goldstein & Hullman (2020). Comprehension accuracy and decision quality rankings can diverge.
Qualification
Evidence base thinner than the frequency literature. May be task-specific. Not all studies find a gap.
Verdict: Directionally important. The practical implication: measure both comprehension and decision quality. When they diverge, choose based on the application's goal.
strong canonical Error bars and confidence intervals are chronically misunderstood — including by researchers.
Support
Belia et al. (2005), Hoekstra et al. (2014). Majority of researchers endorse incorrect CI interpretations.
Qualification
Functional impact may be smaller if users treat CIs as rough 'amount of uncertainty' signals rather than computing precise inferential conclusions.
Verdict: For non-expert interfaces, error bars and CIs should be avoided in favor of more interpretable formats (icon arrays, quantile dotplots).

Design implications grounded in evidence

For Bayesian reasoning tasks, use natural frequencies. Gigerenzer & Hoffrage (1995) raised correct inference from 16% to 46% by changing "the probability of a positive test given the disease is 80%" to "out of every 1,000 people, 10 have the disease; of those 10, 8 test positive." The effect size is large enough that format selection should be a first-order design decision for any interface presenting diagnostic or conditional probability information. The benefit is specific to multi-step integration tasks; for simple probability judgments, the advantage is smaller (Brase, 2008).

For low-numeracy populations, use icon arrays. Garcia-Retamero & Cokely (2013) showed icon arrays (100 person-figures, n colored) substantially improve risk understanding. The benefit is largest for low-numeracy users (~30-50% of the general population per Lipkus, 2007). For non-expert AI interfaces, icon arrays should be the default for binary-outcome risk communication.

For distributional comparisons, quantile dotplots outperform density plots and intervals. Kay et al. (2016) found quantile dotplots (20-100 dots positioned at distribution quantiles) improved probability-of-superiority judgments compared to error bars, density plots, and prediction intervals. The format requires brief onboarding (~30 seconds of explanation) but then performs well across numeracy levels.

Do not use error bars for non-expert audiences. Belia et al. (2005) found many published scientists misinterpret error bars; Hoekstra et al. (2014) found majority of researchers misinterpret 95% CIs. If experts cannot read these formats correctly, using them in public-facing interfaces is a known design error.

Uncertainty Communication

How uncertainty is shown determines how it is used. The same probability, displayed differently, produces measurably different behavior — and the display that best explains the number is often not the one that produces the best decision.

What the evidence shows

Natural frequencies raise correct Bayesian inference from roughly 16% to 46% (Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, 1995) — one of the largest format effects in the judgment literature. The benefit is specific to multi-step integration tasks and depends on preserving the natural sampling structure. For simple probability judgments, the advantage is smaller.

Hofman, Goldstein & Hullman (2020) demonstrated that displays optimized for comprehension and displays optimized for decision quality can rank differently. This comprehension-use gap is the most underappreciated finding in this stream: most research measures whether users understand the display; most applications need users to make better decisions because of the display. These are not the same goal.

Numeracy moderates nearly all format effects. Low-numeracy individuals (~30–50% of the general population by standard measures) are disproportionately harmed by formats requiring arithmetic but benefit most from visual aids like icon arrays (Garcia-Retamero & Cokely, 2013).

Four problems that look like one

Uncertainty communication research and design regularly compresses four distinct problems together. Separating them changes which interventions are relevant.

1
Understanding the display
Can the user correctly decode what the uncertainty representation means? This is a reading comprehension problem.
Most studied. Natural frequencies, icon arrays, and quantile dotplots outperform probability statements and error bars. Comprehension is necessary but not sufficient for good decisions.
2
Acting on the display
Does the display change what the user decides? This is a decision quality problem — distinct from comprehension.
The comprehension-use gap (Hofman et al., 2020) shows these can diverge. The field optimizes mostly for (1) while most applications need (2).
3
Trusting the source
How does the display affect the user's confidence in who or what produced the uncertainty estimate?
Underresearched. Affects AI adoption as much as display format. Confident-sounding AI output inflates source trust regardless of actual accuracy (Steyvers & Peters, 2025).
4
Learning over time
Does repeated exposure to the display help users form better mental models of uncertainty?
Mostly unstudied in naturalistic settings. Animated displays (HOPs) provide experience-based exposure that may support faster calibration, but longitudinal evidence is sparse.

The comprehension-use gap

The core finding

Hofman, Goldstein & Hullman (2020) showed that a display optimized to help users correctly report a probability can rank differently than a display optimized to help users make better choices based on that probability. The display that produces the highest comprehension score is not always the one that produces the best decisions.

This matters because the vast majority of uncertainty communication research measures comprehension as the dependent variable. When designing for decision quality (the application goal), this research base is only partially applicable. Measure both, and when they diverge, choose based on the application's actual goal.

Understudied frontiers

Most uncertainty communication research focuses on single-outcome binary risks (disease probability, weather event). Several important use cases remain underaddressed:

Continuous uncertainty and forecast distributions. Displaying the full distribution of a continuous outcome (e.g., tomorrow's temperature, a model's output range) requires different formats than displaying a single probability. Quantile dotplots are the most promising format, but longitudinal evidence is sparse.

Ranking and comparison under uncertainty. Many AI decisions involve choosing among multiple uncertain options, not just accepting or rejecting one. Format effects in multi-option comparisons are less studied than single-outcome formats.

Long-horizon uncertainty. AI forecasts often cover extended time horizons where probability estimates are inherently wider. Most display research uses short-horizon, well-calibrated forecasts. How displays should adapt for long-horizon, high-variance forecasts remains largely unexplored.

Selective prediction and abstention. AI systems that express uncertainty by withholding a prediction altogether ("I don't know") are a third option beyond numerical confidence scores. The conditions under which abstention outperforms expressed uncertainty are not well characterized.

Design implications grounded in evidence

Choose format based on application goal, not comprehension scores alone. The comprehension-use gap means you need to measure decision quality, not just whether users can read the display. If your application cares about whether users act differently, test for action quality directly.

For Bayesian reasoning tasks, use natural frequencies. Gigerenzer & Hoffrage (1995) raised correct inference from 16% to 46% by converting "the probability of a positive test given the disease is 80%" to "out of every 1,000 people, 10 have the disease; of those 10, 8 test positive." The frequencies must preserve the natural sampling structure to produce the effect — rewritten conditional formats do not always work.

For low-numeracy populations, use icon arrays. Icon arrays (100 person-figures, n colored) substantially improve risk understanding, with the largest benefit for low-numeracy users — roughly 30–50% of the general population. For any public-facing AI interface displaying binary-outcome risk, icon arrays should be the default over probability statements or percentages.

Never use error bars or CIs in public-facing interfaces. Belia et al. (2005) found many published scientists misinterpret error bar overlap; Hoekstra et al. (2014) found majority of researchers hold incorrect beliefs about confidence intervals. Using these formats in non-specialist contexts is a predictable design error.

For verbal probability in AI output, use hybrid formats. "Very likely [>90%]" reduced misinterpretation from ~40% to ~15% in IPCC communication (Budescu et al., 2014). LLM systems that output unanchored verbal probability terms ("probably," "likely") are producing ambiguous communication. Hybrid formats require only prompt-level formatting changes.

Decision Quality & Normative Benchmarks

Every AI advisor embeds assumptions about what "better" means. This stream maps those benchmarks — and what breaks when the wrong one is applied.

What the evidence shows

The qualitative violations of expected utility — certainty effect, reflection effect, framing effects — are among the most replicated findings in behavioral science. Prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979) accounts for these through reference dependence, an S-shaped value function, and nonlinear probability weighting. But the specific parametric values are contested: two 2024 meta-analyses of the loss aversion coefficient λ produced estimates of 1.96 (Brown et al., 607 estimates) and 1.31 (Walasek et al., individual-level refitting). A 2025 re-meta-analysis (Yechiam et al.) found λ≈1.07 when stimulus design artifacts are controlled.

Salience theory (Bordalo, Gennaioli & Shleifer, 2012) explains many of the same anomalies through attention allocation rather than probability distortion. The two models make the same predictions for most standard experiments but diverge in constructed scenarios. Which mechanism is correct has a direct design consequence: if it is attentional, display redesign can reduce anomalies; if it is probability weighting, the distortion persists regardless of display.

What this changes for AI systems

The core design fork

If salience is the mechanism behind choice anomalies, display redesign can fix the problem — making previously non-salient outcome states more visually prominent should shift choices toward expected-utility predictions. If probability weighting is the mechanism, display redesign will not help — the distortion is internal to the decision maker and cannot be corrected by changing what they see.

This is not a subtle academic disagreement. It is a direct decision point for AI system design. Before investing in uncertainty display interventions, the mechanism needs to be tested in your specific domain.

Beyond the salience-weighting question, most AI decision aids implicitly adopt expected utility as the normative standard — optimizing for expected outcome and treating deviations as errors to correct. This is only one of several legitimate evaluation targets. Which one an AI system uses shapes every recommendation it makes.

Five targets for evaluating decision quality

"Decision quality" is underspecified without saying which target you're evaluating against. These five targets are not interchangeable — they can rank decisions differently.

Objective accuracy
Did the decision lead to the correct outcome, relative to a ground truth?
Only applicable when ground truth is available. Most AI decision support implicitly optimizes this.
Expected utility / welfare
Did the decision maximize expected value, given the decision maker's own preferences and probabilities?
The standard economic benchmark. Assumes preferences are stable and well-defined — both are contested.
Regret minimization
Does the decision minimize anticipated regret across possible outcomes?
Loomes & Sugden (1982). Applies when the unchosen outcome is visible. Explains certain preference reversals EU cannot.
Calibration
Are probability assessments well-matched to outcomes over time?
A process target, not an outcome target. Can be good even when individual decisions are wrong. The forecasting literature's primary metric.
Process / autonomy quality
Did the decision maker engage in a cognitively sound process? Were their values respected?
Rarely operationalized but increasingly relevant for AI advisory systems that could override or bypass deliberation.

Key claims

strong canonical People systematically violate expected utility — and the violations are structured.
Support
Allais paradox, reflection effect, isolation effect, framing effects. Robust, replicable, cross-cultural. Kahneman & Tversky (1979) laid out the core patterns; Tversky & Kahneman (1992) extended them to cumulative form.
Qualification
Violations are strongest in lab gambles. In naturalistic environments with feedback, some attenuate. Multiple competing theories explain the same patterns; mechanism remains contested.
Verdict: The qualitative finding is among the most established in behavioral science. Which specific model correctly identifies the mechanism is a separate, still-open question — and the mechanism question is what matters for AI design.
strong debated Loss aversion is real but its magnitude is substantially more variable than canonical estimates suggest.
Support
Brown et al. (2024): λ≈1.96 [1.82, 2.10] across 607 estimates. Walasek et al. (2024): λ≈1.31 [1.10, 1.53] from individual-level refitting. Chapman et al. (2025): 47–57% of a representative sample had λ≤1 (loss-tolerant).
Qualification
Yechiam et al. (2025) found λ≈1.07 when stimulus asymmetry and ordering effects are controlled — near-zero. Heterogeneity across domains, stakes, and methods is substantial. The canonical 2.25 figure is not a reliable design input.
Verdict: Loss framing shifts behavior as a population tendency. The specific ratio cannot be assumed from any published coefficient — test with your population and domain.
mixed debated Salience-based attention, not probability distortion, may drive most choice anomalies.
Support
Bordalo, Gennaioli & Shleifer (2012). The model accounts for most standard violations without invoking nonlinear probability weighting. Divergent predictions from prospect theory exist in constructed scenarios.
Qualification
Empirical track record substantially shorter than prospect theory. Natural data rarely allows a clean test between the two mechanisms.
Verdict: Important precisely because it changes what interventions are worth trying. If correct, the field has been solving the wrong problem — debiasing when it should have been redesigning displays.

Key papers

  • strong canonical Kahneman & Tversky (1979) Prospect theory — dominant descriptive model
  • strong canonical Tversky & Kahneman (1992) Cumulative prospect theory — parametric forms
  • strong debated Brown, Imai, Vieider & Camerer (2024) Largest loss aversion meta-analysis: λ≈1.96
  • mixed debated Walasek, Mullett & Stewart (2024) Individual-level refitting: λ≈1.31
  • mixed emerging Chapman, Snowberg, Wang & Camerer (2025) Representative sample: ~50% are loss-tolerant
  • mixed emerging Yechiam, Zeif & Ashby (2025) Re-meta-analysis: λ≈1.07 when artifacts are controlled
  • mixed debated Bordalo, Gennaioli & Shleifer (2012) Salience theory — attention-based alternative mechanism
  • weak emerging Enke & Graeber (2023) Cognitive uncertainty — anomalies as noise

Design implications grounded in evidence

Test the salience mechanism before investing in debiasing. A controlled experiment varying visual salience of outcome states while holding probability information constant distinguishes the mechanisms directly. If choices shift toward expected-utility predictions, salience is operative and display redesign is your intervention. If they don't shift, the distortion is internal and debiasing training is a higher-cost, lower-certainty route.

Loss framing shifts behavior — but the magnitude needs local calibration. Presenting AI failure rates as "18% error rate" vs. "82% accuracy rate" will produce different responses. The effect size varies enough across studies (λ ranging from 1.07 to 1.96 depending on methodology) that assuming the canonical ratio is a design error. Test your specific population and domain.

Pick the right evaluation target before measuring "decision quality." An AI system optimized for expected accuracy will behave differently than one optimized for calibration or regret minimization. For high-stakes irreversible decisions, regret-minimization framing may better reflect user preferences. For repeated decisions with feedback, calibration is the more tractable target. Being explicit about which target is embedded in the system is itself a design choice.

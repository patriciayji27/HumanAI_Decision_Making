Decision Quality & Normative Benchmarks

Every AI advisor embeds assumptions about what 'better' means. Expected utility theory provides the benchmark most systems implicitly adopt. Prospect theory shows why real choices deviate. The gap between them is where design problems live.

Assessment

The qualitative violations of expected utility — certainty effect, reflection effect, framing effects — are among the most replicated findings in behavioral science. Prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979) accounts for these through reference dependence, an S-shaped value function, and nonlinear probability weighting. But the specific parametric values are contested: two 2024 meta-analyses of the loss aversion coefficient λ produced estimates of 1.96 (Brown et al., 607 estimates) and 1.31 (Walasek et al., individual-level refitting). A 2025 re-meta-analysis (Yechiam et al.) found λ≈1.07 when stimulus design artifacts are controlled.

Salience theory (Bordalo, Gennaioli & Shleifer, 2012) explains many of the same anomalies through attention allocation rather than probability distortion: people overweight the states where options differ most, not the states with extreme probabilities. The two models make the same predictions for most standard experiments but diverge in constructed scenarios. Which mechanism is correct has practical implications: if it is attentional, display redesign can reduce anomalies; if it is probability weighting, the distortion persists regardless of display.

Key claims

strong canonical People systematically violate expected utility — and the violations are structured.
Support
Allais paradox, reflection effect, isolation effect, framing effects. Robust, replicable, cross-cultural.
Qualification
Violations are strongest in lab gambles. In naturalistic environments with feedback, some attenuate. Multiple competing theories explain the same patterns.
Verdict: The qualitative finding is among the most established in behavioral science. Whether any specific model correctly identifies the mechanism is a separate, still-open question.
strong debated Loss aversion is real but its magnitude is more variable than the canonical ratio suggests.
Support
The qualitative pattern is robust across dozens of studies. Chapman et al. (2025) found 47-57% of a representative sample is loss-tolerant (λ≤1).
Qualification
Meta-analyses disagree: Brown et al. (2024) find λ≈1.96; Walasek et al. (2024) find λ≈1.31; Yechiam et al. (2025) find λ≈1.07 under controlled conditions. Heterogeneity across domains, stakes, and measurement methods is substantial.
Verdict: Losses loom larger than gains as a population tendency. The precise ratio is contested and context-dependent. For design, assume loss aversion exists but test with your specific population.

Design implications grounded in evidence

Loss framing effects are real but the magnitude depends on methodology. Two 2024 meta-analyses bracket the loss aversion coefficient: Brown et al. found λ≈1.96 [1.82, 2.10] across 607 estimates; Walasek et al. found λ≈1.31 [1.10, 1.53] from individual-level model fitting. For interface design, this means loss framing will shift user behavior — presenting AI failure rates as "18% error rate" vs. "82% accuracy rate" will produce different responses — but the exact magnitude cannot be assumed from any single published coefficient. Test with your population.

The salience-vs-weighting distinction has a concrete design test. If salience theory is correct, making a previously non-salient payoff state more visually prominent should change choices. If probability weighting is correct, visual prominence should not matter — the distortion is internal. A controlled experiment varying visual salience of outcome states while holding probability information constant would distinguish the mechanisms and directly inform whether display redesign can reduce anomalies in a specific interface.

If cognitive uncertainty (Enke & Graeber, 2023) generalizes, AI can reduce preference noise through structured comparison. The construct predicts that within-person inconsistency is partly driven by uncertainty about one's own valuations. An AI that helps users compare options along explicit dimensions — rather than presenting a single recommendation — may reduce noise without overriding any specific preference. This is testable: compare decision consistency (repeated identical choices) with and without AI-structured comparison aids.

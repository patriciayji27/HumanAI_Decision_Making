Research Archive

When humans and AI agents make decisions together under uncertainty, what actually determines whether the outcome is better or worse than either alone?

This archive maps what cognitive science and AI research have established, where the real disagreements are, and what follows for designing systems where humans and agents collaborate on uncertain decisions. Organized by research direction, not by date.

Questions driving this archive
Stream 8 · AI Agents
When does AI uncertainty communication actually improve human decisions?
The field has shifted from "are LLMs calibrated?" to "does expressing calibration help the user?" The answer depends on explanation style, trust dynamics, and decision protocol — not just accuracy.
Stream 1 · Belief Elicitation
Can conversational AI elicit beliefs better than forms?
Dialogue-based elicitation could adapt to the person's cognitive state in real time. But it might also anchor responses, create false coherence, or introduce social dynamics that distort reporting. Empirical evidence is almost absent.
Stream 3 · Decision Quality
What should "better" mean when an AI helps you decide?
If the benchmark is expected utility, the AI imposes a model the person may not endorse. If the benchmark is revealed preference, the data is contaminated by framing effects. Every AI advisor embeds assumptions about what you should want.
Stream 2 · Uncertainty Communication
Why do displays that improve comprehension sometimes worsen decisions?
Understanding a probability distribution and acting well on it are different cognitive tasks. Optimizing for one can degrade the other. The field hasn't solved which to target — and the answer depends on context.
Stream 4 · Attention & Salience
Is the AI explanation problem actually an attention problem?
Salience theory suggests many "irrational" choices are really attention failures. If so, fixing the interface — not retraining the person — is the right intervention. But distinguishing attentional effects from probability weighting is hard.
Stream 7 · Sampling & Experience
Does experiencing uncertainty (sampling) produce better decisions than reading about it?
The description-experience gap is one of the most robust findings: people treat described probabilities very differently from experienced ones. But the experience format underweights rare events — which can be catastrophic.
Frontier debates the field hasn't settled
Position A

Probability weighting explains choice anomalies — people systematically distort probabilities, and this distortion is a stable cognitive feature.

vs
Position B

Salience and attention explain the same anomalies — people overweight whichever payoffs grab attention, and the "distortion" is context-dependent, not stable.

Position A

Comprehension-first design: make the user understand the uncertainty deeply, and good decisions will follow.

vs
Position B

Action-first design: optimize the display for decision quality directly — full understanding isn't needed and sometimes hurts.

Position A

AI explanations help: showing confidence, reasoning, and feature importance improves human-AI team performance.

vs
Position B

AI explanations mislead: fluent explanations increase trust independently of accuracy, and wrong explanations hurt more than right ones help.

Papers that shaped the field
Settled
Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk
Kahneman & Tversky, 1979
Still the dominant descriptive model. Loss aversion, reference dependence, and probability weighting remain the framework most applied work builds on — even as salience theory and cognitive uncertainty offer alternative mechanisms.
Settled
How to Solve the Base-Rate Problem Using Natural Frequencies
Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, 1995
Established that format causally affects reasoning, not just comprehension. Natural frequencies improve Bayesian inference from ~15% to ~50% correct. The mechanism is debated but the effect is rock-solid.
Frontier
Cognitive Uncertainty
Enke & Graeber, 2023
Introduced a measurable construct linking attention limits to choice quality. Potentially unifies many behavioral anomalies as "noise from uncertainty about your own preferences" rather than stable biases. Too early to know if it holds broadly.

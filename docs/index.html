Human-Agent Decision Making Under Uncertainty

A research archive at the intersection of cognitive science and AI agents, organized by research direction. Covers belief elicitation, uncertainty communication, decision quality benchmarks, attention and salience, risk and ambiguity, temporal dynamics, sampling and experience, and AI-human collaboration.

Research streams
AI Agents as Decision Partners
Calibrated AI confidence does not reliably improve human decisions — explanation style, fluency, and decision protocol matter at least as much.
Decision Quality & Normative Benchmarks
Whether the mechanism behind choice anomalies is probability weighting or salience-driven attention determines whether display redesign can fix the problem.
Uncertainty Communication
Displays that improve comprehension sometimes worsen decisions — and most research measures comprehension while most applications need decision quality.
Belief Elicitation & Calibration
Elicitation is partly generative. The method shapes the belief it claims to measure, and there may be no single ground-truth internal probability to recover.
Attention, Memory & Salience
Salience theory offers an attention-based account of choice anomalies — and if correct, the intervention (interface redesign) is cheaper than debiasing training.
Risk, Ambiguity & Controllability
AI is a source of ambiguity (unknown error distribution), not risk (known probability). Ambiguity aversion — not risk aversion — drives adoption resistance.
Temporal Dynamics
Evaluation frequency is a design variable with causal effects on risk-taking. Myopic loss aversion has the cleanest design implications in this archive.
Sampling, Granularity & Experience
Described and experienced probabilities produce opposite rare-event biases. Most AI systems are pure description — and hybrid formats remain underused.
Frontier tensions

Probability weighting (prospect theory) explains choice anomalies as stable cognitive distortions.

vs

Salience-driven attention (Bordalo et al.) explains the same anomalies as context-dependent overweighting of prominent payoffs.

Comprehension-first design: help users understand uncertainty deeply and good decisions follow.

vs

Action-first design: optimize for decision quality directly — full understanding is unnecessary and sometimes counterproductive.

AI explanations reduce overreliance by helping users assess AI reliability and override errors.

vs

Incorrect AI explanations hurt more than correct ones help — the expected value of explanation features is negative when accuracy is imperfect.

Papers that shaped the field
Strong Canonical
Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk
Kahneman & Tversky, 1979
Dominant descriptive model. Reference dependence, loss aversion, and probability weighting remain the framework most applied work builds on.
The qualitative effects are robust. The λ≈2.25 estimate shows more heterogeneity than textbooks suggest — recent meta-analyses range from 1.31 to 1.96. Salience theory competes on mechanism.
Strong Canonical
Natural Frequencies Improve Bayesian Reasoning
Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, 1995
Format causally affects reasoning performance. Natural frequencies raise correct Bayesian inference from ~15% to ~50%.
Robust across dozens of replications. Benefit is strongest for multi-step Bayesian problems; smaller for simple judgments. The evolved-processor interpretation is debated.
Strong Emerging
When Combinations of Humans and AI Are Useful
Vaccaro, Almaatouq & Malone, 2024
Meta-analysis of human-AI team performance. Complementarity is achieved in a minority of studied conditions. Task difficulty and AI accuracy are the primary moderators.
Publication bias may understate the failure rate. The conditions under which complementarity succeeds are more specific than the field has assumed.
